Cutting down on ultra-processed foods

In a metabolic study by the US health agency NIH, 20 adults on an ultra-processed diet ate 508 kilocalories more per day and gained 0.9 kilos in two weeks. Observational data on almost 10 million people link a high intake of ultra-processed foods with cardiovascular death, type 2 diabetes and mental illness. In a British study from 2025, 50 adults lost twice as much weight with minimally processed food as with an ultra-processed diet following the same dietary guidelines.

In the UK, according to the authors of the UPDATE study, more than half of the energy consumed comes from ultra-processed foods, and the situation is similar in the USA and in Europe. Eating less of them is one of the most discussed pieces of dietary advice in recent years, and unlike many trends, there are not only observational data on it but also controlled feeding studies. This page shows what they support and where the classification becomes blurred.

In short

In a metabolic study by the US health agency NIH, 20 adults on an ultra-processed diet ate 508 kilocalories more per day than on an unprocessed one, although the meals offered were matched for calories, sugar, fat, salt and fiber; in two weeks they gained 0.9 kilos. In the British UPDATE study from 2025, 50 adults lost 2.1 percent of their body weight with minimally processed food and 1.1 percent with an ultra-processed diet following the same dietary guidelines. An umbrella review in the BMJ with almost 9.9 million people links a high intake of ultra-processed foods with 50 percent more cardiovascular deaths, more type 2 diabetes and more anxiety disorders. The catches: for hard endpoints there are only observational data, mostly of low quality, and experts often classify the same products differently.

What counts as ultra-processed

The common definition comes from the NOVA classification of the group around Carlos Monteiro at the University of São Paulo. It sorts foods into four groups by the extent and purpose of industrial processing: unprocessed or minimally processed foods, processed culinary ingredients such as oil or sugar, processed foods and ultra-processed foods. The fourth group is created by breaking foods down into individual substances, chemically modifying them and recombining them, often with cosmetic additives and elaborate packaging.

The practical test that the NOVA authors themselves propose: does the ingredient list contain at least one ingredient that is rarely used in the kitchen, such as glucose-fructose syrup, hydrogenated or interesterified fats or hydrolyzed protein? Or an additive that shapes taste and appearance, that is, flavorings, flavor enhancers, colorings, emulsifiers, sweeteners, thickeners? Then the product counts as ultra-processed. On sweeteners and sugar substitutes, see also Erythritol and Xylitol.

How it might work

The obvious suspicion is that ultra-processed foods simply contain more sugar, fat and salt. The 2019 NIH study eliminated exactly that: the meals offered were matched for calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fiber, and yet participants ate markedly more on the ultra-processed diet, mainly carbohydrates and fat, but not protein.

Several pathways are discussed as explanations. The 2025 Lancet series names high energy density, very high palatability, soft texture and a destroyed food structure as drivers of overeating, plus fewer protective plant compounds and more contaminants and additive mixtures. In a meta-analysis of ten randomized trials, eating rate was the clearest difference: ultra-processed food was eaten faster. And the extra intake depended on whether the ultra-processed diet was more energy-dense. When both were equally energy-dense, it largely disappeared.

What is well supported

The short-term effect on eating behavior is the best supported: under controlled conditions, people eat more on an ultra-processed diet and gain weight, and on a minimally processed diet they lose weight, in the NIH study 0.9 kilos in two weeks in each case. The British UPDATE study confirmed this in 2025 over eight weeks in everyday life, with meals that followed the official dietary recommendations in both phases. It is also supported that high intake is very consistently associated with poorer health data in observational studies: in the BMJ review, associations were found for 32 of 45 endpoints studied, from mortality to type 2 diabetes to depression.

What the studies show

NIH metabolic study, Cell Metabolism 2019

20 weight-stable adults lived on the research ward of the US health agency NIH and received, in random order, two weeks each of an ultra-processed and an unprocessed diet, as much or as little as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet, they ate 508 kilocalories more per day, 280 of them from carbohydrates and 230 from fat. They gained 0.9 kilos, and lost 0.9 kilos on the unprocessed diet. Weight closely followed energy intake.

UPDATE study, Nature Medicine 2025

55 adults in England with overweight who had previously obtained at least half of their energy from ultra-processed foods received eight weeks each of minimally processed and ultra-processed food deliveries, both put together according to the official British dietary guidelines; 50 were included in the analysis. Both diets lowered weight, the minimally processed one by 2.1 percent, the ultra-processed one by 1.1 percent. Fat mass, triglycerides and cravings fell more with the minimally processed diet, whereas LDL cholesterol fell more with the ultra-processed one. The study was funded by a medical foundation and a non-governmental organization, without industry involvement.

Feeding study in men, Cell Metabolism 2025

Men ate ultra-processed and unprocessed diets in a crossover design, each with a normal and with an increased amount of calories. On the ultra-processed diet, body weight and the ratio of LDL to HDL rose, regardless of the amount of calories. Follicle-stimulating hormone fell, sperm motility tended to decrease, and in the blood there was a trend toward more of a phthalate breakdown product.

Meta-analysis of randomized trials, 2026

Ten randomized trials, ultra-processed versus less processed. Weight gain and eating rate were significantly higher on the ultra-processed diet, energy intake tended to be higher, but depending on the statistical model not always significantly. When both diets had the same energy density, practically no difference in energy intake was seen any longer. The authors rate the certainty of the evidence as low and point out that the diets in the previous studies were not matched for nutrients.

Umbrella review, BMJ 2024

45 pooled analyses from observational studies with a combined almost 9.9 million people. The authors rated as convincing the associations with cardiovascular death, with a 50 percent higher risk, with type 2 diabetes, with a 12 percent higher risk in the dose-response analysis, and with anxiety disorders and common mental disorders. Highly suggestive was a 21 percent higher risk of death overall. According to GRADE, however, only 4 of the 45 analyses were of moderate quality, 22 of low and 19 of very low quality.

Lancet series, 2025

The first paper of a three-part Lancet series summarizes more than 100 prospective studies, meta-analyses, randomized trials and mechanistic studies and concludes that the displacement of traditional dietary patterns by ultra-processed foods is a major driver of diet-related chronic diseases. The first author is Carlos Monteiro, who also wrote the 2019 NOVA guide.

How consistently NOVA is applied, European Journal of Clinical Nutrition 2022

French nutrition experts were asked to assign 120 branded products with ingredient lists and 111 generic foods to the NOVA groups. Agreement between the raters was low, with a kappa of 0.32 and 0.34 on a scale up to 1, even when the ingredient list was available. For some of the products, the assignment was unambiguous; for others, it was spread across all four groups.

Where the data stop

For heart attack, cancer or mortality, there are only observational data, and according to GRADE these are predominantly of low or very low quality. People who eat a lot of ultra-processed food also differ in many other ways; this can only partly be adjusted for statistically. The randomized trials are small, run for two to eight weeks and measure weight and risk factors, not diseases.

What exactly has the effect is also open. Part of the overeating can be explained by energy density and eating rate, and in the 2026 meta-analysis it largely disappeared when both diets were equally energy-dense. Whether processing itself is harmful or is mainly a feature that coincides with unfavorable nutrients has not been settled. Added to this is the blurred classification: if experts classify the same products alike only with a kappa of 0.32, the measurement of intake in large studies is also uncertain. And UPDATE shows that an ultra-processed diet following dietary recommendations does not automatically perform badly: it also lowered weight and LDL cholesterol, only the weight less strongly.

How to do it

Read the ingredient list. According to the NOVA guide, a single typical ingredient is enough to classify a product as ultra-processed: glucose-fructose syrup, hydrogenated fats, hydrolyzed protein or additives such as flavorings, flavor enhancers, colorings, emulsifiers, sweeteners and thickeners. A short list of ingredients that you also have in your kitchen is the opposite.

Start with what you eat most often and replace it with dishes made from basic ingredients. In UPDATE, the advantage of minimally processed food was about one percent of body weight in eight weeks, in people who had previously obtained at least half of their energy from ultra-processed foods. The lever is therefore greatest where the share is high.

Eat more slowly. Eating rate was the clearest difference between the diets in the randomized trials. And do not take the classification too precisely: if even experts disagree on whether a product belongs in group 3 or 4, arguing about it is not worth it. What matters is the overall share of fresh and minimally processed foods. Anyone also interested in carbohydrates will find the data on this under ketogenic diet.

Safety

Eating fewer ultra-processed foods usually means more food made from basic ingredients; no serious risks have been described for this. In UPDATE, mild gastrointestinal complaints were common under both diets. Anyone who sorts foods only by degree of processing may, however, discard products that perform well by other standards: in UPDATE, LDL cholesterol fell more under the ultra-processed diet following dietary recommendations than under the minimally processed one.

Frequently asked questions about ultra-processed foods

What exactly are ultra-processed foods?

According to the NOVA classification, products that are recombined from broken-down and modified food components, often with additives for taste and appearance. As a rule of thumb: if the ingredient list contains at least one ingredient that is rarely used in the kitchen, such as glucose-fructose syrup or hydrolyzed protein, or an additive such as flavorings, emulsifiers, sweeteners or thickeners, the product counts as ultra-processed.

Do ultra-processed foods make you fat?

In controlled studies, they lead to eating more. In the NIH study, 20 adults ate 508 kilocalories more per day with them and gained 0.9 kilos in two weeks. In UPDATE, people lost 2.1 percent in eight weeks with minimally processed food and 1.1 percent with ultra-processed food. A meta-analysis of ten studies found more weight gain, but rates the certainty of the evidence as low.

Is it just about sugar, fat and salt?

Not only. In the NIH study, the meals offered were matched for calories, sugar, fat, sodium and fiber, and yet participants ate more on the ultra-processed diet. A 2026 meta-analysis, however, attributes a large share to energy density: when both diets were equally energy-dense, the extra intake largely disappeared.

How well supported is the link with diseases?

It is very consistent, but almost only observed. The BMJ review with almost 9.9 million people found associations for 32 of 45 endpoints, including 50 percent more cardiovascular deaths. According to GRADE, only 4 of the 45 analyses were of moderate quality, the rest of low or very low quality. There are no randomized trials on diseases.

Are all ultra-processed foods equally unfavorable?

The data argue against it. In UPDATE, an ultra-processed diet following dietary recommendations also lowered weight, and LDL cholesterol even fell more with it. In addition, experts often classify the same products differently, in a French study with an agreement of only 0.32 on a scale up to 1.

How do I recognize ultra-processed foods in the supermarket?

By the ingredient list, not by the advertising on the package. Look for ingredients you do not use at home and for additives such as flavorings, flavor enhancers, colorings, emulsifiers, sweeteners and thickeners. The shorter the list and the more familiar the ingredients, the more likely the product is minimally processed.
