Filtered water with minerals

German tap water is strictly monitored. An added benefit of filters has not been established – poorly maintained filters can do harm.

Reverse osmosis, remineralization, spring water in glass bottles: there is a large market around drinking water. The starting question, however, is a different one – namely, whether tap water in Germany has a problem at all.

In short

German tap water is strictly monitored: in the 2020 to 2022 reporting period, up to 120,000 measurements per parameter from 2,507 water supply areas were analyzed, and the drinking water met all quality requirements in more than 99 percent of cases. No study has shown an added health benefit of filters; the German Environment Agency even advises against filter devices in the building’s plumbing, because bacteria can multiply in cartridges that are not changed. Reverse osmosis followed by remineralization with table salt does not work chemically. The one case in which action is justified is lead pipes in your own building’s plumbing – and there no filter helps, only replacement.

What is behind it

The data basis is unusually broad. The report of the German Environment Agency relies on up to 120,000 measurements per parameter from 2,507 water supply areas. Almost all of the 14 microbiological and chemical parameters examined were complied with in more than 99 percent of cases. The highest exceedance rate concerned coliform bacteria as an indicator parameter, at 1.1 percent of samples in 2022 – indicator bacteria whose detection triggers a follow-up examination and does not automatically mean a health hazard.

The weak point is rarely in the network. Exceedances of the limit for nitrate were the rare exception, and for lead they occurred mainly at the consumer’s tap, that is, in the building’s plumbing. That is exactly where an individual check makes sense.

Why filtering is not the solution

Activated carbon and ion exchange filters remove chlorine, flavor substances and hardness minerals. A physiological benefit of this is not defined, because the starting quality already meets the limits. The mechanism that actually is described runs in the other direction: filters retain organic material that serves bacteria as a breeding ground, and they stand at room temperature. The German Environment Agency therefore writes verbatim that it advises against the use of these devices.

The Verbraucherzentrale (German consumer advice centre) reaches the same conclusion and states that from a health perspective the use of filters for tap water is generally not necessary; Stiftung Warentest rated water filters in 2022 as superfluous to harmful. Claims about harmonizing or energizing water cannot be scientifically substantiated.

Demineralizing and remineralizing

Reverse osmosis removes almost all dissolved substances, including calcium and magnesium. The WHO has summarized the consequences of permanently very low-mineral water in a chapter of its own: increased diuresis with greater excretion of sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium and magnesium, losses of up to 60 percent of magnesium and calcium during cooking, because low-mineral water draws these substances out of the food being cooked, and a corrosive effect on pipe materials with increased uptake of heavy metals. As desirable, the chapter names at least 10 mg/l magnesium and 20–30 mg/l calcium.

Remineralization with salt does not solve this. According to tests by Stiftung Warentest, Himalayan salt consists of 97 to 99 percent sodium chloride, ordinary table salt of about 98 percent; despite the advertising with 84 minerals, the Bavarian State Office for Health and Food Safety found only eight additional minerals, mostly in very small amounts. Anyone who adds table salt to demineralized water adds sodium, not calcium or magnesium.

What is well supported

Above all, the starting situation is robust: more than 99 percent compliance with quality requirements, based on up to 120,000 measurements per parameter in 2,507 supply areas, on the legal basis of the German Drinking Water Ordinance (Trinkwasserverordnung). The official assessment of filters is equally robust: the German Environment Agency advises against devices in the building’s plumbing because of possible microbial contamination, and the Verbraucherzentrale considers them generally not necessary from a health perspective. And the consequences of permanently demineralized water are reliably described in the WHO compilation, along with the target values for magnesium and calcium.

What the studies show

Does drinking more do anything at all

Systematic review of randomized trials on daily fluid intake: of 1,464 records screened, 18 studies met the inclusion criteria, with intervention durations between 4 days and 5 years. Positive findings were reported for weight loss and for kidney stones, with 15 fewer events per 100 participants over 5 years. Ten of the 18 studies reported at least one positive result, eight negative ones. The authors’ conclusion: the quantity and quality of the evidence are limited.

Water and weight in overweight people

Meta-analysis of eight randomized trials: body weight WMD −0.33 kg (95 % CI −1.75 to 1.08; I² = 78 %), BMI −0.23 kg/m² (95 % CI −0.55 to 0.09), waist circumference 0.05 cm (95 % CI −1.20 to 1.30). None of the three endpoints reached significance.

The counterposition to the WHO assessment

The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment has assessed mineral water with very low mineral content and concludes that with a balanced diet and normal fluid intake, no side effects are recognizable that would argue against its use. It recommends caution with a very unbalanced diet, for vegans and people with lactose intolerance, for pregnant and breastfeeding women, and for athletes with exercise lasting more than 1.5 hours.

Where the data stop

There is no study that has tested filtered against unfiltered German tap water on a health endpoint. The statement that an added benefit has not been established refers to this absence and not to a negative finding. Conversely, the warning about microbial contamination is also an official assessment and not a quantified risk estimate: how often it actually happens and with what consequences, the source does not say. For reverse osmosis in the home followed by remineralization, there is no study with a health endpoint – neither for benefit nor for harm.

The WHO target values for magnesium and calcium also come from a review chapter, not from randomized trials, and they are intended for the drinking water supply of entire populations. On microplastics in drinking water, no study could be found that links a health endpoint in humans to intake via drinking water: figures on particle concentrations exist, effect data do not.

How to do it

Check instead of filter is the robust short version. Let the first water in the morning run until it comes out of the tap cool – especially with chrome-plated fittings, from which nickel can leach, and in older installations. Have the building’s plumbing checked if the building was built before 1973 or lead pipes are suspected; that is the justified case for an analysis, not for a filter device. If you filter for taste reasons, change the cartridge according to the manufacturer’s instructions, store the water cool and use it promptly. On quantity: the DGE gives as a guideline for total water intake 2,600 ml/day for 25- to 51-year-olds, of which 1,410 ml come from beverages; the rest comes from solid food and oxidation water. The common two to three liters of drinking water are considerably higher.

Safety

With lead pipes the matter is clear: water from them is always unsuitable for pregnant women, infants and children up to the age of six, and complete replacement of the pipes is absolutely necessary. Filters are not a solution for this. For people with a weakened immune system, the risk of microbial contamination from cartridges that are not changed is more relevant than for healthy people. And drinking too much is a real, if rare, risk: a systematic review of 177 publications with 590 patients found a median intake of 8 liters per day and a median serum sodium of 118 mmol/l at presentation; 31 percent had no underlying condition. With heart or kidney failure, the fluid intake set by a physician applies in any case.

Frequently asked questions about Filtered water with minerals

Is German tap water really that good?

According to the data of the German Environment Agency, yes. In the 2020 to 2022 reporting period, up to 120,000 measurements per parameter from 2,507 supply areas were analyzed; the drinking water met all quality requirements in more than 99 percent of cases. The highest exceedance rate concerned coliform indicator bacteria, at 1.1 percent of samples. The weak point is usually not in the network but in your own building’s plumbing.

Does a water filter do anything?

No health benefit has been established, and both relevant bodies advise against them: the German Environment Agency warns of bacterial growth in filters that are not changed and advises against devices in the building’s plumbing; Stiftung Warentest rated water filters in 2022 as superfluous to harmful. If you filter for taste reasons, change the cartridge on time and store the water cool and use it promptly.

What about reverse osmosis and remineralization?

For this combination in the home there is no study with a health endpoint. For permanently very low-mineral water, the WHO describes increased electrolyte excretion and cooking losses of up to 60 percent for magnesium and calcium, and names as desirable at least 10 milligrams of magnesium and 20 to 30 milligrams of calcium per liter. The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment sees no recognizable side effects of very low-mineral water with a balanced diet, but names risk groups.

Does a pinch of Himalayan salt replace the removed minerals?

No. According to tests by Stiftung Warentest, Himalayan salt consists of 97 to 99 percent sodium chloride, ordinary table salt of about 98 percent. Despite the advertising with 84 minerals, the Bavarian State Office for Health and Food Safety found only eight additional minerals, mostly in very small amounts. With the exception of sodium and chloride, Himalayan salt does not cover the daily mineral requirement.

How much should I drink?

As a guideline for 25- to 51-year-olds, the DGE gives a total water intake of 2,600 milliliters per day, of which 1,410 milliliters come from beverages; the rest comes from solid food and metabolism itself. The common recommendation of two to three liters of drinking water is considerably higher. That drinking more brings health benefits is established in randomized trials only in isolated cases, most likely for kidney stones.

Can you drink too much?

Yes – rare, but real. A systematic review of 590 documented cases of hyponatremia from water intake found a median intake of 8 liters per day and a median serum sodium of 118 millimoles per liter at presentation. 31 percent of those affected had no underlying condition. With heart or kidney failure, the fluid intake set by a physician applies in any case.
