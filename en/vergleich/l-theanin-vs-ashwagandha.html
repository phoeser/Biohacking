L-theanine vs. Ashwagandha

L-theanine or ashwagandha – what demonstrably helps with stress?

The short answer

Both are searched for the same purpose, but what is proven differs. L-theanine has the broader database: a meta-analysis of 31 randomised trials with 1,168 participants found better choice reaction time after a single dose (SMD 0.51), i.e. an acute effect on attention. In a meta-analysis of 15 studies with 873 adults, ashwagandha lowered anxiety, stress and cortisol scores after eight weeks. Where the data end: L-theanine's stress effect is smaller (SMD 0.31) and comes mainly from studies with a high risk of bias. With ashwagandha, quality of life did not improve, there are no data beyond eight weeks, and Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) warns of rare liver damage. There is no direct comparison of the two.

Why these numbers

L-theanine

An unusually broad database for a nootropic: a meta-analysis of 31 RCTs with 1,168 participants (Molecular Psychiatry 2026) finds an improvement in choice reaction time of SMD 0.51 after a 200 milligram single dose; two further meta-analyses (Nutrition Reviews 2025, Journal of Clinical Medicine 2025) point in the same direction. The stress effect (SMD 0.31) is carried mainly by studies with a high risk of bias; anxiety and fatigue remain inconsistent. The mixing ratio with caffeine was never tested against another: every study set exactly one dose pair against placebo, and in 2018 EFSA attributed the attention effect of black tea to caffeine alone. The GABA narrative comes from animal data; alpha waves in the EEG are a surrogate. The effects are real, but smaller and measured over shorter periods than advertised.

Ashwagandha

There are several RCTs on stress and cortisol, mostly small, short and close to manufacturers. The mechanism remains vague. An important point for the safety data: reports of liver damage have occupied several authorities.

Frequently asked questions

Which helps more with stress: L-theanine or ashwagandha?

They have never been compared directly. On stress scales the finding is clearer for ashwagandha: after eight weeks the mean difference was −4.88 on the perceived stress scale and −2.36 for cortisol, but quality of life did not improve. For L-theanine, the acute stress effect was smaller at SMD 0.31 and rests mainly on studies with a high risk of bias; attention is better supported there. For ashwagandha, no benefit has been shown for a clinically diagnosed anxiety disorder.

How quickly do L-theanine and ashwagandha work?

The studies look at very different time frames. L-theanine was studied mainly acutely, with measurements usually taken 30 to 60 minutes after a single administration; how daily intake over months plays out is open. The ashwagandha data refer to eight weeks. Some users report calm on the first or second day, others after one to two weeks – these are reports, not proof of effect.

How safe are L-theanine and ashwagandha?

L-theanine was consistently well tolerated in the studies; the meta-analysis of 31 trials reports no serious adverse events, and in one study reports of headache increased. For ashwagandha, the largest study with 1002 people over eight weeks was unremarkable. The BfR nevertheless warns of rare liver damage up to acute liver failure, derives no safe guidance value and advises children, pregnant and breastfeeding women and people with liver disease against it.

Do L-theanine or ashwagandha help with sleep?

For L-theanine there is a dedicated meta-analysis of 19 studies with 897 participants. It found small improvements in subjective time to fall asleep (SMD 0.15), daytime sleepiness (SMD 0.33) and overall sleep quality (SMD 0.43) – all questionnaire scores, not sleep-lab measurements. For ashwagandha, calmer sleep is mainly a common user report; the meta-analysis evaluated concerns anxiety, stress, cortisol and quality of life, not sleep.
