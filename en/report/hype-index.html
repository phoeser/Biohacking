Hype Index Q4 2026: where marketing runs ahead of the data

Paul Höser, Biohacking Kompakt · Edition Q4 2026 · As of: 2026-11-28 · published quarterly

For 268 of 527 supplements, peptides, medicines and practices in our database – just over half – the marketing promises considerably more than the studies show. 53 remedies (10%) have never been studied in humans. The gap is widest for research peptides and bioregulators; for 80 entries (15%), by contrast, the advertising stays largely in line with the data, among them creatine, strength training and several approved medicines.

By area: peptides and bioregulators furthest ahead

The gap between marketing and data is not spread evenly. Among peptides and experimental substances, 133 of 224 entries (59%) have a hype gap of 3 or less, among the Russian bioregulators 13 of 14. For supplements the figure is 42%, for practices such as infusions, cold or light therapy 53%, and for everyday tips 33%.

What changed since the database review on 2026-11-27

On 2026-11-27, all entries were checked against new studies. 11 scores changed, and every change raised a value (human evidence 7×, safety data 5×, hype gap 1×): new studies, larger samples, more safety data. A higher value means better studied, not better – for nicotinamide riboside and ivermectin the additional data speak against the advertised benefit.

What the Hype Index measures – and what it does not

It measures how far the marketing of a remedy runs ahead of its data. The basis is the BK-Score axis “hype gap” (0 to 10; 10 means advertising in line with the data, 0 to 2 promises without a basis, 3 to 5 a single finding turned into a general claim). Counted are entries with a value of 3 or less – the same threshold at which the label “Hype far ahead of evidence” is assigned. “Never studied in humans” is the number of entries with the label “Not studied in humans”.

It does not measure whether something works or is safe. Like the whole BK-Score, the index rates the state of knowledge, not the substance: a low hype gap does not mean “ineffective” or “dangerous”, a high one does not mean “recommended”. Nor does it measure the volume of advertising or market size: a niche peptide counts as much as vitamin D. The database covers what is discussed in biohacking, not a representative sample of the market.

Why 268 and not 52? The label follows fixed priority rules: an entry with good or thin human evidence is labeled by its evidence first, even if its marketing is far ahead. The index counts the axis directly and therefore catches overhyping at every level of evidence.

Reproducible: all figures come from scores.json (as of 2026-11-28, 527 entries; one practice appears in two areas). The comparison uses the version before the review on 2026-11-27. The assessment is a disclosed estimate by Biohacking Kompakt according to the published rules, not a scientific evaluation and not a recommendation.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Hype Index?

A quarterly figure from Biohacking Kompakt: the share of rated biohacking remedies whose marketing runs far ahead of the data (hype gap of 3 or less on a scale of 0 to 10). In edition Q4 2026 this applies to 268 of 527 entries (51 percent).

How many biohacking remedies were never studied in humans?

53 of 527 rated remedies (10 percent) carry the label “Not studied in humans”; most of them are research peptides (44) and bioregulators (5).

Does a low hype gap mean a remedy is useless?

No. The index rates the state of knowledge and how far marketing runs ahead of it, not whether something works or is safe. Even well-studied medicines can be heavily overhyped.
