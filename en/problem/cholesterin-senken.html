Question & Context

Lowering LDL cholesterol – what helps?

In brief

LDL cholesterol can be lowered noticeably through diet, and the effects add up. Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated ones, soluble fiber from oats, barley or psyllium, plant sterols and nuts each lower LDL by a few percent. Combined, such a diet came close to the effect of the statin lovastatin in a small study. Among supplements, red yeast rice lowers LDL the most because it contains a statin itself, with the same risks. Berberine and bergamot have weaker data, and omega-3 mainly lowers triglycerides. With very high levels, early heart disease in the family or a high overall risk, medication belongs on the table, decided together with a doctor.

What matters first

Replace saturated fats

The Cochrane review of 15 randomized trials with 56,675 participants found that eating less saturated fat for at least two years lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17 percent. The more cholesterol fell, the larger the effect; there was little effect on overall mortality. Polyunsaturated fats, for example from vegetable oils, or carbohydrates worked as replacements. In practice this means less butter, fatty meat and sausage, and more vegetable oil, nuts and seeds.

Soluble fiber: psyllium, oats, barley

Viscous fibers bind bile acids in the gut, and the liver pulls cholesterol from the blood to make new ones. A meta-analysis of 28 trials with 1,924 participants found that psyllium husk lowered LDL by 0.33 mmol/L, with moderate-quality evidence. Beta-glucan from oats and barley works on the same principle, and EFSA recognizes its cholesterol-lowering effect.

Plant sterols and nuts

Plant sterols and stanols compete with cholesterol for absorption in the gut. In an analysis of 124 studies they lowered LDL by an average of 6 to 12 percent, depending on the amount; two of the three authors worked for a food company. In a pooled analysis of 25 trials, nuts lowered LDL by 7.4 percent, most strongly in people with high starting levels, and the type of nut made little difference.

Combine everything

The effects add up. In a study of 46 adults with raised blood lipids, a diet with plant sterols, soy protein, soluble fiber and almonds lowered LDL by 28.6 percent in one month, the statin lovastatin by 30.9 percent and a low-fat control diet by 8.0 percent. The study was small and short, and the food was provided; how much of the effect remains in everyday life depends on how well you stick to it.

When you should have this checked by a doctor

Have your blood lipids assessed by a doctor if your LDL is very high, around 190 milligrams per deciliter or more, if close relatives had a heart attack or stroke at a young age, or if you notice yellowish fatty deposits on tendons or eyelids. This can point to familial hypercholesterolemia: it affects about one in 313 people, but about one in fourteen among people with very high LDL. In that case treatment belongs in medical hands, and relatives should have their levels checked too. Also talk to your practice before taking red yeast rice, berberine or similar products alongside prescribed medication, and get in touch if you develop muscle pain, muscle weakness or dark urine while taking statins or red yeast rice. Chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, paralysis or speech problems are an emergency: call the emergency number 112 immediately. Please never stop prescribed cholesterol medication on your own.

Why LDL matters – and where Lp(a) comes in

LDL particles carry cholesterol in the blood. When they are present in high numbers for years, they build up in the vessel wall and drive atherosclerosis. What counts is the level and the duration of exposure, not a single reading. How urgent a raised LDL is also depends on your other risk factors: blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, kidney function and family history.

Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a) for short, is a separate cholesterol-rich particle whose level is largely inherited and which is not measured in a standard cholesterol check. The European Atherosclerosis Society regards it as a causal risk factor for cardiovascular disease and aortic valve stenosis, even when LDL is very low, and recommends measuring it at least once in adults. There are no specific lowering treatments yet; if the level is high, the panel recommends tackling the other risk factors early and consistently.

Red yeast rice, berberine, bergamot, omega-3: what the studies show

Red yeast rice lowers LDL more than any other supplement, and the reason is simple: according to EFSA, its active compound monacolin K is identical to lovastatin, a prescription statin. A meta-analysis of 20 studies found a reduction of 1.02 mmol/L compared with placebo, with no difference from statins; the safety data in the studies, however, were mostly of low quality. In 2018 EFSA was unable to name any intake that is free of health concerns and describes severe adverse effects on muscles and the liver, with individual cases even at low intakes. Taking red yeast rice therefore means taking a statin without medical supervision.

In a meta-analysis of 16 trials with 2,147 participants, berberine lowered LDL by 0.38 mmol/L, but the trials were mostly of poor methodological quality. In 9 randomized trials with 615 participants, bergamot extract improved LDL, total cholesterol and triglycerides; six of the nine trials were rated as poor quality, and the certainty of the evidence as low to very low. For both, data on heart attacks and mortality are missing.

Omega-3 fatty acids are a remedy for high triglycerides, not for high LDL. In high-strength prescription form they lower triglycerides by 30 percent or more. Products containing EPA and DHA can even raise LDL in people with very high triglycerides; pure EPA did not.

Exercise, weight and medication

Losing weight and exercising regularly belong in every plan for the heart: they improve blood pressure, blood sugar and triglycerides and so lower overall risk. LDL itself, however, responds mainly to what you eat, so training and weight loss do not replace the dietary levers above, they complement them.

If diet and lifestyle are not enough or the risk is high, there are well-studied medications. Statins have the strongest evidence, with data from large outcome trials. Ezetimibe blocks cholesterol absorption in the gut and is often combined with a statin; PCSK9 inhibitors given as injections and bempedoic acid are options mainly for very high risk or statin intolerance. Which option fits depends on your overall risk and is a decision for your doctor.

  • Lipoprotein(a) Evidence 8/10 · Well studied – effect not confirmedA genetically determined blood lipid that is missing from the standard cholesterol check and is well established as a risk marker. A drug designed to lower it, however, showed no benefit in the large HORIZON trial.
  • Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) Evidence 8/10 · Well supportedWell studied, including large outcome trials whose results partly contradict each other. Among blood lipids it mainly affects triglycerides, hardly LDL.
  • Red yeast rice Evidence 7/10 · Supported, with caveatsLowers LDL clearly in meta-analyses because monacolin K is identical to the statin lovastatin. EFSA was unable to set a safe daily intake in 2018 and 2025.
  • Beta-glucan Evidence 7/10 · Supported, with caveatsOat and barley beta-glucan lowers LDL in many randomized trials, and EFSA recognizes the effect. The effect is moderate and was measured in studies lasting a few weeks.
  • Berberine Evidence 6/10 · Supported, with caveatsLowers blood lipids and blood sugar in many, mostly methodologically weak trials; outcome data are missing. In a 2026 draft, EFSA could not identify a safe intake.
  • Citrus Bergamot Evidence 5/10 · Thin human evidenceLowers LDL in small studies, these too with manufacturer involvement; the meta-analysis rates the evidence as very low. Heart attacks and mortality have never been studied.
  • Black garlic Evidence 4/10 · Thin human evidenceMarketed for heart and cholesterol, but in the studies the usual blood lipids mostly stayed unchanged. The data point more to a small drop in blood pressure.

Frequently asked questions

How fast can I lower my LDL with diet?

In studies, effects appear within a few weeks. A diet with plant sterols, soy, soluble fiber and almonds lowered LDL by 28.6 percent within one month. Single components work less strongly, usually in the range of a few percent. A follow-up test after a few weeks shows how strongly you personally respond.

Is red yeast rice a natural alternative to statins?

No, it contains a statin itself: monacolin K is identical to lovastatin. That is why it lowers LDL in a similar way and can also cause similar side effects on muscles and the liver. EFSA was unable to set an intake free of health concerns. Do not take it alongside statins or other medication without talking to your doctor.

Does eating oats help with high cholesterol?

Yes, to a moderate extent. Beta-glucan from oats binds bile acids in the gut, and the liver takes cholesterol from the blood to replace them. Many randomized trials show a reduction in LDL, and EFSA recognizes the effect. Psyllium husk works on the same principle. Such building blocks achieve the most when combined with eating less saturated fat.

Does omega-3 help with high cholesterol?

Hardly against high LDL. Omega-3 fatty acids mainly lower triglycerides, by 30 percent or more in high-strength prescription form. Products with EPA and DHA can even raise LDL in people with very high triglycerides. If your triglycerides are raised, discuss treatment with your doctor.

What is lipoprotein(a) and should I have it measured?

Lipoprotein(a) is a cholesterol-rich particle whose level is largely inherited. It is considered an independent risk factor for heart attack and aortic valve stenosis, even when LDL is low. The European Atherosclerosis Society recommends measuring it at least once in adults, especially with familial hypercholesterolemia or early heart disease in the family.

When do I need cholesterol-lowering medication?

That depends not on LDL alone but on your overall risk: age, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, kidney function, family history and existing vascular disease. With very high levels, suspected familial hypercholesterolemia or after a heart attack, medication is usually part of treatment. You decide together with your doctor; diet works on top of it.
