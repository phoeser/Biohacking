Tremella

Mushroom · Tremella fuciformis, silver ear, snow mushroom, snow fungus, white jelly mushroom, Tremella polysaccharides

Tremella, also called silver ear or snow mushroom, is a gelatinous edible mushroom that has been eaten in Asia for centuries. In the West it is marketed mainly as a beauty mushroom, as a plant-based alternative to hyaluronic acid. For the skin, there is so far no human study on oral intake, but there are first small studies on memory and blood sugar.

In short

Tremella fuciformis is an edible mushroom whose polysaccharides bind a lot of water. Applied topically, Tremella extract demonstrably moisturizes. Whether capsules or powder improve the skin from within has not been studied in humans; this is shown only in animal experiments. Another finding is more interesting: in a small study with 75 people, 600 or 1,200 mg of Tremella per day over eight weeks improved memory complaints and test performance more than placebo. The catch is the thin data: few, small and short studies, no long-term data.

What it is

Tremella fuciformis is a white, frilly jelly fungus that grows on wood. In Asia it has been used for centuries as an edible and medicinal mushroom, usually dried and soaked before cooking. For cultivation it needs a partner fungus that breaks down the wood for it. In the German guidelines for edible mushrooms, it is on the list of common species as Silberohr.

As a dietary supplement, powder or extract from the fruiting body is usually sold, often under names such as snow mushroom or as a beauty mushroom. In cosmetics, Tremella extract is found in creams and serums. These are two different applications with different data.

New are products with isolated components, such as purified chondroitin sulfate from the fruiting body, which is planned for dietary supplements at up to 500 mg per day. Such isolates are legally something different from mushroom powder and require their own authorization in the EU.

How it is supposed to work

The most important constituent is Tremella polysaccharides. Unlike the beta-glucans from shiitake or oats, this is an acidic heteropolysaccharide built mainly from mannose, xylose and glucuronic acid. These sugar chains bind a lot of water and form gels. This is the origin of the idea of plant-based hyaluronic acid.

On the skin this is plausible, because a moisture film acts directly there. When swallowed, the route is different. The large sugar chains are not transported unchanged to the skin, but end up in the large intestine, where they are fermented by bacteria. A 2026 review therefore describes the plausible route via the gut flora: fermentation, short-chain fatty acids, less inflammation in the body and, through this, perhaps a better skin barrier. In mice with an atopic dermatitis model, Tremella polysaccharides even worked better when swallowed than when applied topically, accompanied by more regulatory T cells and an altered gut flora. In cell and animal studies, the polysaccharides also show antioxidant, blood sugar-lowering and blood lipid-lowering effects. Unlike shiitake or oyster mushrooms, however, Tremella provides hardly any ergothioneine, only traces in the range of micrograms per gram.

What users are looking for

Most people reach for Tremella for plumper, better-hydrated skin, often together with collagen or hyaluronic acid. Some use it for memory or simply as a traditional food. Many report softer, less dry skin after a few weeks. These are user reports that should be taken seriously, but no controlled study has confirmed them so far.

What is well supported

Surprisingly, what is best supported is not the skin but memory. In a double-blind study, 75 adults with subjective memory problems took 600 mg or 1,200 mg of Tremella per day or placebo for eight weeks. The Tremella groups reported significantly fewer memory complaints, the primary goal of the study, and also performed better in tests of short-term memory and planning. Side effects did not occur more often than with placebo.

There is also a first signal for metabolism. 56 overweight people with prediabetes drank a Tremella beverage or placebo daily for twelve weeks. In the Tremella group, long-term blood sugar (HbA1c) fell slightly from 6.03 to 5.96 percent, and waist circumference from 95.2 to 93.46 cm. Topically, the moisturizing effect has been shown: a hand gel with 10 percent Tremella extract kept the skin of 20 volunteers moister than a gel without extract until the end of the measurement after 180 minutes.

What the studies show

The memory study

75 adults with subjective memory complaints received 600 mg or 1,200 mg of Tremella per day or placebo for eight weeks, 30 each in the Tremella groups and 15 in the placebo group. Complaints, short-term memory and executive functions improved more than with placebo. Brain imaging showed volume differences in individual regions, but this was an exploratory additional analysis.

The prediabetes study

56 overweight adults with prediabetes drank a Tremella beverage or placebo for twelve weeks, 28 per group. In the Tremella group, HbA1c and waist circumference decreased slightly. However, the authors only compared before and after within the groups; they do not report a direct comparison with placebo. The study was funded by a food company.

The atopic dermatitis mouse

Mice with an artificially induced atopic dermatitis-like inflammation received Tremella polysaccharides applied topically or in their feed. Both routes reduced water loss, skin thickening and swelling; swallowed, they worked better. This is an animal model and shows a possible route, not an effect in humans.

Where the data stop

For the main promise, the skin from within, there is not a single human study. A 2026 review notes that oral hyaluronic acid has been tested on human skin, whereas Tremella polysaccharides have not. The evidence comes from animal experiments, cell culture and topical cosmetics. I did not find the widespread advertising claim that Tremella binds many times its weight in water in any primary study. And even high water binding in a glass says nothing about what reaches the skin after swallowing. Some manufacturers speak of mushroom-based hyaluronic acid; so far this has only been studied in a laboratory model.

The other findings are narrow too. The memory study is small, its primary endpoint is a questionnaire, and it has not been replicated. A second study tested a combination drink of fish roe, Tremella and yeast, whose effect cannot be attributed to the mushroom. The blood sugar data are exploratory. There are no long-term data and no studies on longevity endpoints.

Status, approval and legal

According to the EU Novel Food Catalogue, the fruiting body of Tremella fuciformis is not considered novel; it is an ordinary food and may be sold as powder or extract in dietary supplements. In Germany, Belgium and Spain it is on national positive lists for edible mushrooms. The mycelium, that is, the fungal network, is a different matter: it is considered a novel food without authorization. This also applies to isolated chondroitin sulfate from Tremella, which was classified as novel in 2025. There is no authorized EU health claim for Tremella, so advertising skin or memory effects is not permitted for foods. Tremella is not on the WADA Prohibited List 2026.

Safety

In the studies over eight to twelve weeks, side effects did not occur more often than with placebo. However, systematic safety data and long-term studies are lacking, and I found no case reports of harm from Tremella products. A real risk concerns the kitchen: fresh mushrooms or mushrooms soaked for too long can be colonized by the bacterium Burkholderia gladioli. Its toxin-producing variant generates bongkrekic acid, for which there is no antidote. In Shanghai, 16.6 percent of fresh Tremella samples carried the bacterium, but the toxin-producing variant was found there only in dried wood ear (mu-err). A report by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention advises against eating mushrooms soaked for 24 hours or longer. Interactions with medicines have not been studied in humans. Because animal data point to a lowering of blood sugar, anyone taking diabetes medication should discuss this with a doctor. There are no data for pregnancy and breastfeeding, and no official maximum amounts either.

Frequently asked questions about Tremella

Is Tremella better than hyaluronic acid for the skin?

That cannot be said, because there is no human skin study on Tremella capsules. Oral hyaluronic acid has been studied on human skin; Tremella so far only in animal experiments and as a cream. Applied topically, Tremella extract moisturizes.

Does Tremella powder work on the skin from within?

That is not established. In mice, swallowed Tremella polysaccharides improved the skin barrier, presumably via the gut flora. A study in humans with skin moisture as an endpoint is lacking.

Does Tremella help memory?

In a small study with 75 people, 600 or 1,200 mg per day over eight weeks improved memory complaints and test results more than placebo. That is an interesting signal, but it is a single small study that has not yet been replicated.

Is Tremella permitted in Germany?

Yes. In the EU, the fruiting body is considered an ordinary food and is listed in the German guidelines for edible mushrooms as Silberohr. The mycelium and isolated chondroitin sulfate from Tremella, by contrast, are considered novel foods without authorization.

Does Tremella have side effects?

In studies over eight to twelve weeks, not more often than placebo; long-term data are lacking. When cooking: do not soak the mushroom for 24 hours or longer and discard spoiled produce, because a dangerous bacterial toxin can form there.

What is the difference between Tremella and shiitake?

Shiitake mainly contains beta-glucans such as lentinan, which target immune cells. Tremella contains a differently structured, acidic polysaccharide made of mannose, xylose and glucuronic acid that binds a lot of water. Accordingly, the mushrooms are marketed for different purposes.
