Treatment & Procedure

Micronutrient counseling

Focus

Micronutrient counseling is meant to clarify which vitamins and minerals you are actually lacking, and to clear the rest out of the cupboard. Targeted supplementation for a proven deficiency or in certain phases of life is well supported. The broad lab check in people without symptoms and the pill for everyone are much less so.

In short

Micronutrient supplementation works where there is a deficiency or a clear increased need: folic acid when trying to conceive markedly lowers the risk of neural tube defects, and iron relieves perceived fatigue when iron stores are empty. In a large long-term study in men, a multivitamin slightly lowered the cancer rate, but the US preventive services authority considers the evidence insufficient overall. According to the IGeL-Monitor, laboratory screening for vitamin D or B12 deficiency without symptoms has an unclear benefit. Good counseling therefore measures selectively, checks for interactions and pays attention to upper limits.

What counseling should achieve

It starts with diet, phase of life, symptoms and medications. Only from these does it follow which lab values make sense. The goal is a short plan: what you eat differently, what you supplement and for how long, and when to measure again.

Often the most valuable part is crossing things off. Anyone who combines several products quickly takes individual substances several times over. Counseling that sorts out overlapping products and checks interactions with medications prevents overdoses and saves money.

Where supplementation clearly makes sense

Pregnancy and the wish to conceive are the textbook case. The German Nutrition Society (DGE) recommends that women who want to become or could become pregnant take 400 micrograms of folic acid per day as a supplement, starting at least 4 weeks before pregnancy. The reason is well supported, see below.

A second case is a proven deficiency with symptoms. Empty iron stores with fatigue, or a vitamin D deficiency in people with risk factors, for whom two thirds of guidelines recommend a measurement: here, measuring and correcting selectively makes sense. If there is a well-founded suspicion, statutory health insurance pays for the test.

What it costs

A vitamin D test without suspicion is a self-pay service. The IGeL-Monitor puts the laboratory costs at 27.98 to 32.18 euros, plus consultation and blood sampling. Extensive micronutrient profiles cost correspondingly more. Ask beforehand which values will be measured and why.

How to recognize a reputable offer

Good counseling justifies every measurement, says openly when a value would not change the recommendation, and does not sell you its own product in the same appointment. It names upper limits and duration of intake, schedules a follow-up and, in the case of unexplained exhaustion, first recommends having the cause clarified by a physician.

What is well supported

The strongest evidence is for folic acid when trying to conceive. A Cochrane review of 5 studies with 6,708 births found a relative risk of neural tube defects of 0.31 with daily folic acid, with high-quality evidence.

In iron deficiency without anemia, iron reduced self-reported fatigue in a meta-analysis of 18 studies. And in the Physicians Health Study II with 14,641 men aged 50 and over, a daily multivitamin over a median of 11.2 years slightly lowered the cancer rate, a hazard ratio of 0.92.

What the studies show

Physicians Health Study II

Double-blind randomized trial with 14,641 male US physicians aged 50 and over, multivitamin versus placebo, median 11.2 years. The primary endpoint, total cancer, was narrowly met, a hazard ratio of 0.92. Prostate and colorectal cancer as well as cancer mortality did not differ significantly.

Cochrane: folic acid before and in early pregnancy

Review of 5 randomized trials with 6,708 births. Folic acid, alone or with other micronutrients, markedly reduced neural tube defects, a relative risk of 0.31. No effect was seen on other malformations or miscarriages.

Iron for fatigue without anemia

Meta-analysis of 18 randomized trials with 1,170 participants. Self-reported fatigue decreased with iron; objectively measured physical performance stayed the same.

US preventive services authority 2022

The Task Force assessed the evidence for non-pregnant adults. It explicitly recommends against beta-carotene and vitamin E for the prevention of cardiovascular disease and cancer. For multivitamins and most single nutrients, it considers the evidence insufficient to weigh benefits and harms.

Where the data stop

For the early detection of vitamin D or vitamin B12 deficiency in people without symptoms, the IGeL-Monitor found no studies showing a benefit and rates both as unclear. A review of 31 guidelines found not a single one that recommends vitamin D screening of the general population.

For individually compiled supplement plans based on large micronutrient profiles, no controlled studies were found. And the positive multivitamin study concerned well-nourished men with a small effect; this cannot be transferred at will.

Status, approval and legal

In Germany, food supplements are foods. There are no binding maximum amounts for vitamins and minerals either nationally or at EU level; the proposals of the German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) serve as guidance. Measurements without suspicion are self-pay services; if there is a well-founded suspicion, statutory health insurance pays.

Safety

More is not better. Beta-carotene as a preventive supplement tends to do more harm than good. For vitamin B6, the EFSA upper limit is 12 milligrams per day; above that, long-term intake risks nerve damage. The BfR considers vitamin D up to 20 micrograms, that is 800 IU per day, unproblematic even without a prior measurement, and considerably higher doses only after a confirmed deficiency and on medical advice. Iron should be supplemented only for a proven deficiency. During pregnancy, with kidney or liver disease and while taking medication, agree any supplementation with a physician.

Frequently asked questions about micronutrient counseling

Do I need a multivitamin?

Most well-nourished adults do not necessarily need one. A large study in men found a slightly lower cancer rate, but the US preventive services authority considers the evidence insufficient overall. Targeted supplementation for a proven deficiency makes more sense.

Should I have my vitamin D level measured?

Without symptoms and risk factors, the benefit is unclear according to the IGeL-Monitor, and no guideline recommends screening the general population. If there is a well-founded suspicion, statutory health insurance pays for the test. According to the BfR, vitamin D up to 20 micrograms per day is considered unproblematic even without a measurement.

Which vitamins do I need during pregnancy?

Folic acid is well supported. The DGE recommends 400 micrograms per day for women who want to conceive, starting at least 4 weeks before pregnancy and continuing through the first trimester. Any further supplements should be agreed with a physician.

Does iron help against fatigue?

If iron stores are empty, yes: even without anemia, perceived fatigue decreased in a meta-analysis. Measurable physical performance did not change. Without a proven deficiency, iron should not be supplemented.

What does a micronutrient analysis cost?

According to the IGeL-Monitor, the laboratory costs for a single vitamin D value alone are 27.98 to 32.18 euros, plus consultation and blood sampling. Large profiles cost correspondingly more. Ask beforehand which values will be measured and why.

Can you overdose on vitamins?

Yes. For vitamin B6 there is an upper limit of 12 milligrams per day, and beta-carotene as a preventive supplement tends to do harm. Anyone combining several products should have the totals checked.
