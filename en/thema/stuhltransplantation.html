Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Biohacking

In a faecal transplant, technically faecal microbiota transfer, processed stool from a healthy donor is placed into the gut of a patient. What sounds odd at first is a highly effective, well-supported treatment for recurrent gut infection with Clostridioides difficile, recommended by guidelines and approved as a medicinal product in the US since 2022. In the longevity scene, the transfer is discussed as “microbiome rejuvenation”. There are impressive mouse data for this, but in humans so far only small, short-term studies on metabolism.

In short

In recurrent C. difficile infection, a Cochrane review of six randomized trials with 320 adults found that the infection resolved almost twice as often with faecal transfer as with the comparison treatment; statistically, one in three treated patients benefits in addition. The European guideline prefers it from the second recurrence onwards, and two products, Rebyota and Vowst, are approved in the US. For metabolism, nine small randomized trials showed slightly better blood glucose and insulin values in the first six weeks, no difference after twelve weeks and none for weight. The rejuvenation idea rests on mice with accelerated ageing. In Germany, donor stool is a medicinal product and belongs in the hands of a physician, because pathogens can be transmitted along with the stool.

What it is and how it works

Trillions of bacteria, viruses and fungi live in the gut, the gut flora or microbiota. A diverse gut flora occupies niches and nutrients and thereby keeps pathogens in check. Antibiotics can disturb this balance; specialists call it dysbiosis. In this gap, Clostridioides difficile can spread, a bacterium that produces toxins and causes severe, sometimes life-threatening diarrhoea. In almost a third of those infected the infection comes back, and every further course of antibiotics weakens the gut flora again (Minkoff 2023).

The faecal transfer targets the cause: instead of killing the pathogen over and over, it restores a complete, healthy community of gut bacteria. The donor stool is processed and, depending on the centre, given by colonoscopy, enema, a tube into the small intestine or as a capsule. The two US products are standardised further developments of this: Rebyota is a microbiota preparation obtained from donor stool, and Vowst contains live bacteria from screened stool donations in capsules taken by mouth.

Established medicine: recurrent C. difficile infection

Here the transfer is not an experiment but part of the guidelines. Since its 2021 update, the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) prefers faecal transfer or the antibody bezlotoxumab, each in addition to standard antibiotic treatment, for the second and every further recurrence. What decides the choice of treatment is the risk of recurrence, not the severity of the disease alone (van Prehn 2021).

In Germany, according to the BfArM, the transfer is used in a very large number of hospitals, mainly for exactly this indication. In the US, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Rebyota on November 30, 2022 and, on April 26, 2023, Vowst as the first faecal microbiota product taken by mouth, both to prevent a further recurrence in adults after antibiotic treatment of a recurrent infection. These approvals are the strongest evidence that a targeted replacement of the gut flora can be a real, measurable therapy.

The idea of microbiome rejuvenation

It is understandable why the longevity scene is paying attention: if a donor’s gut flora can end a stubborn infection, perhaps it could also reverse age-related changes in the gut flora. The best-known basis is a study in Nature Medicine. Two mouse models of progeria, a hereditary disease with strongly accelerated ageing, had an altered gut flora, with more Proteobacteria and fewer Verrucomicrobia. In people with progeria the researchers also found dysbiosis, whereas centenarians had strikingly many Verrucomicrobia. When the progeria mice received the gut flora of healthy mice, they lived longer and healthier; giving the single bacterium Akkermansia muciniphila was already enough for beneficial effects (Bárcena 2019). More on this bacterium under Akkermansia.

The second line is metabolism. In 2012, a Dutch research group transferred the gut flora of lean donors to 18 men with metabolic syndrome; six weeks later, insulin sensitivity was higher than after transfer of their own stool. A meta-analysis has pooled this and other small randomized trials (Qiu 2023). If you are more interested in measuring your own gut flora or in bacterial preparations, see microbiome tests and probiotics.

What is well supported

The benefit in recurrent C. difficile infection is very well supported. Across six randomized trials, the 2023 Cochrane review found a cure rate with faecal transfer almost twice as high as with comparison treatments, usually vancomycin, relative risk 1.92, with moderate certainty. Serious adverse events were not more frequent, if anything less frequent. Guidelines recommend the transfer, and two products are approved in the US.

It is also supported that metabolism can be influenced in the short term via the gut flora: in randomized trials with placebo or the patient’s own stool as comparison, fasting glucose, long-term blood glucose, insulin and HDL cholesterol were somewhat more favourable in the first weeks after the transfer. That is a real signal of a causal contribution of the gut flora, even if the effects were small.

What the studies show

Minkoff 2023 (Cochrane) – recurrent C. difficile infection

Six randomized trials with 320 adults from Denmark, the Netherlands, Canada, Italy and the US, almost exclusively without a weakened immune system. The transfer was given by tube, enema or colonoscopy; the comparison groups mostly received vancomycin. The infection resolved almost twice as often with transfer (relative risk 1.92), number needed to treat 3, moderate certainty. Serious adverse events (relative risk 0.73) and deaths (0.57) tended to be less frequent, with few events and wide confidence intervals. The data allow no conclusion for people with weakened immunity.

Vowst – pivotal trial

In the randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 89 adults received Vowst and 93 placebo, each after antibiotic treatment of a recurrence. Within eight weeks, the infection came back in 12.4 percent with Vowst and in 39.8 percent with placebo. Side effects more frequent than with placebo were bloating, fatigue, constipation, chills and diarrhoea (FDA 2023).

Qiu 2023 – metabolism and overweight

Meta-analysis of nine randomized trials with 303 people with overweight or metabolic syndrome, compared with placebo or their own stool. Up to six weeks after the transfer, fasting glucose (minus 0.12 mmol/l), HbA1c (minus 0.37 mmol/mol, roughly 0.03 percentage points) and insulin were lower and HDL cholesterol slightly higher. From twelve weeks on, there was no difference in any value, except for a slightly lower HbA1c in a single study. There was no difference in weight at any time. No serious adverse events occurred; the certainty of the results was mostly low.

Bárcena 2019 – mice with accelerated ageing

In two mouse models of progeria, the gut flora of healthy mice extended healthspan and lifespan; giving Akkermansia muciniphila alone also had beneficial effects. The researchers saw the restoration of secondary bile acids as a possible mechanism. The model is a rare hereditary disease, not normal ageing, and the approach has not been tested in humans.

What users report

The following figures come from surveys and are self-reports, not proof of efficacy. In a long-term follow-up from five US centres, 77 patients with recurrent C. difficile infection answered a questionnaire, on average 17 months after transfer by colonoscopy. They had, on average, five unsuccessful courses of antibiotics behind them. In 74 percent, the diarrhoea was gone within three days at most, and 53 percent said they would choose the transfer as the first treatment if the infection recurred (Brandt 2012).

An online survey reached 84 people who had done a faecal transfer on their own, 80 percent on themselves. The most common reasons were inflammatory bowel disease (35 percent) and irritable bowel syndrome (29 percent), conditions for which there is little evidence. 82 percent reported an improvement, 12 percent side effects. Respondents had been reached via foundation and social media pages; people with bad experiences are probably under-represented (Ekekezie 2020).

Where the data stop

For the longevity promise, there is not a single human study that targeted ageing, lifespan or markers of ageing. The mouse data come from a model with pathologically accelerated ageing, and whether the gut flora of a young or healthy donor takes hold in healthy older people and changes anything is an open question. For metabolism, the randomized trials tend to argue against a lasting effect: the improvements were small and gone after twelve weeks, and weight did not change.

The established part also has limits. The Cochrane review included almost only people with an intact immune system; randomized data for people with weakened immunity are lacking. In the Cochrane authors’ assessment, long-term effects have not been studied sufficiently, and large registries would be needed for that. In Brandt’s follow-up, four patients developed conditions after the transfer whose relationship to it is unclear. For inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome, the reasons for most self-experiments, there is little evidence according to the survey authors.

Status, approval and legal

In Germany, donor stool for treating humans is a medicinal product under Section 2(1) of the German Medicinal Products Act (AMG). It is prepared in the hospitals under the direct professional responsibility of the treating physician; this licence-exempt preparation must be notified to the competent state supervisory authority under Section 67 AMG. Clinical trials with faecal transfer are approved by the BfArM, and only with comprehensive, multi-stage donor selection and testing, including multidrug-resistant pathogens and SARS-CoV-2.

According to the BfArM in 2023, there was no ready-made medicinal product based on stool authorised in the EU. In the US, Rebyota and Vowst have been approved since 2022 and 2023, exclusively to prevent further recurrences of C. difficile infection in adults. There is no approval for metabolism, weight or longevity in either the EU or the US.

Safety

In the controlled trials on C. difficile infection, the most common complaints were mild and affected the gastrointestinal tract; serious adverse events were not more frequent than with comparison treatments. The fundamental risk lies in the material itself: bacteria, viruses and parasites can be transmitted with the stool. In 2019, two recipients from two different trials in the US developed a bloodstream infection with multidrug-resistant, ESBL-producing E. coli; genome sequencing traced both cases to the same donor, whose stool had not been tested for such pathogens. One patient died (DeFilipp 2019). For the approved product Vowst, too, the FDA points out that despite tested donors it may transmit infectious agents and may contain food allergens.

The BfArM therefore requires multi-stage donor screening with a detailed questionnaire and repeated blood and stool tests for bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi and multidrug-resistant pathogens. It also names the possible risk of triggering unwanted immunological, metabolic or tumour-related processes in the recipient. Self-experiments at home bypass exactly this protection: in Ekekezie’s survey, 92 percent knew their donor, and most relied on information from the internet. If you are considering a faecal transfer, discuss it with a gastroenterology or infectious diseases clinic.

Frequently asked questions about faecal microbiota transplantation

How well does a faecal transplant work against Clostridioides difficile?

Very well when the infection keeps coming back. In a Cochrane review of six randomized trials with 320 adults, the infection resolved almost twice as often with faecal transfer as with the comparison treatment, usually vancomycin. Statistically, one in three treated patients benefits in addition. The European guideline prefers the transfer from the second recurrence onwards.

Does a faecal transplant rejuvenate the body?

This has not been studied in humans. The idea comes from animal experiments: mice with a hereditary disease that makes them age prematurely lived longer and healthier after receiving the gut microbiota of healthy mice. There is no study in humans that targeted ageing, lifespan or markers of ageing.

Does a faecal transplant help with weight loss or diabetes?

Not with weight: in nine randomized trials with 303 people with overweight or metabolic syndrome, there was no difference from the sham treatment. Blood glucose, insulin and HDL cholesterol were slightly better in the first six weeks; from twelve weeks on the difference had disappeared. That does not make the faecal transfer a diabetes treatment.

Is a faecal transplant allowed in Germany?

Yes, under a physician’s responsibility. Donor stool used for treatment is a medicinal product in Germany. Hospitals prepare it themselves under medical responsibility, mainly for recurrent C. difficile infection, and must notify the state authority; clinical trials are approved by the BfArM. According to the BfArM in 2023, there was no ready-made product authorised in the EU.

What are the risks of a faecal transplant?

In the short term mostly mild complaints such as diarrhoea or abdominal cramps. The serious issue is the possible transmission of pathogens: in 2019, two recipients in the US developed a bloodstream infection with multidrug-resistant E. coli from the same donor, and one died. This is why the BfArM requires extensive donor screening. Long-term effects have not been studied sufficiently.

Can you do a faecal transplant yourself?

It happens, but it is risky. In an online survey of 84 people who had tried it on their own, 92 percent knew their donor, and most wanted to treat inflammatory bowel disease or irritable bowel syndrome. Without the multi-stage donor screening with blood and stool tests, exactly the protection that is meant to prevent transmission is lost.
