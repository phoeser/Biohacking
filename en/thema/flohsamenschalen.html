Psyllium husk (psyllium)

Herbs · Psyllium, Plantago ovata, ispaghula

11 min read · 10 sources, checked November 7, 2026

In the BK-Score: Well supported – human evidence 8 out of 10, safety data 8 out of 10. The data in humans are solid.

Psyllium husk is one of the best-supported herbal remedies of all: it makes stools softer and more regular and lowers LDL cholesterol, as shown in meta-analyses of randomized trials and recognized by the European Medicines Agency. When it comes to weight loss and its reputation as a “natural Ozempic”, the evidence becomes much thinner.

What psyllium husk is

Psyllium husk is the seed husk of blond plantain, Plantago ovata, in English also called psyllium husk or ispaghula. It consists mostly of soluble fiber that swells with water into a viscous gel and is barely fermented in the gut. It is sold as whole or ground husks, as a powder, in capsules and as an approved herbal medicine.

In the BK-Score, human evidence stands at 8 out of 10 points. The axis rates the state of knowledge: for digestion and cholesterol there are many randomized trials, for heart attacks or strokes as endpoints there are none. Related topics are beta-glucan, another gel-forming fiber with a similar LDL effect, red yeast rice and probiotics; the page Lowering cholesterol gives an overview.

How it works

With fluid, the husk forms a gel that holds water in the stool. The stool becomes softer and bulkier, which stretches the bowel wall and stimulates bowel movement; according to the EMA’s Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC), the effect sets in after 12 to 24 hours.

In the small intestine, the gel makes the chyme more viscous and so slows the breakdown and absorption of nutrients, including sugar from the meal. How exactly this leads to the lowering of cholesterol is not fully understood, according to the HMPC.

What is well supported

  • LDL falls, in two independent analyses. A meta-analysis of 28 randomized trials with 1,924 participants found, at a median of around 10.2 g per day, LDL −0.33 mmol/l, non-HDL cholesterol −0.39 mmol/l and ApoB −0.05 g/l; for ApoB the authors rated the evidence as high. A second analysis of 29 trials arrived at LDL −0.35 mmol/l.
  • Effective in chronic constipation. Across 16 randomized trials, 66 percent responded to fiber, compared with 41 percent under control. Psyllium was among the types with a significant effect.
  • Helps with irritable bowel syndrome. Under ispaghula, symptoms persisted less often than under placebo; the relative risk was 0.78.
  • Officially recognized. The HMPC classifies psyllium husk as “well-established use”, that is, supported by studies, for constipation, for cases in which a soft stool is desired, for irritable bowel syndrome with constipation and as an adjunct to diet in elevated cholesterol. In the US, a health claim on coronary heart disease is authorized.

What the studies show

Jovanovski 2018: LDL, non-HDL and ApoB

The meta-analysis in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition included 28 randomized trials lasting at least three weeks with 1,924 participants, with and without elevated cholesterol. At a median of around 10.2 g of psyllium per day, LDL fell by 0.33 mmol/l (95% interval −0.38 to −0.27), non-HDL cholesterol by 0.39 mmol/l and ApoB by 0.05 g/l. Because of inconsistent individual results, the team rated LDL and non-HDL with moderate certainty and ApoB with high certainty. An analysis by Zhu 2024 of 29 trials with 2,769 participants on Plantago species confirmed the direction and found no effect on HDL and triglycerides.

van der Schoot 2022: fiber in chronic constipation

The meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials with 1,251 adults found a response rate of 66 versus 41 percent (RR 1.48; 1.17 to 1.88). More bowel movements and better consistency appeared mainly at more than 10 g of fiber per day and from four weeks of duration; psyllium and pectin had significant effects. The authors urge caution because of high heterogeneity. Bloating occurred more often under fiber.

Ford 2008: irritable bowel syndrome

In the British Medical Journal, Ford and colleagues analyzed 12 trials with 591 patients with irritable bowel syndrome. Across all types of fiber, the relative risk of persistent symptoms was 0.87 (0.76 to 1.00), that is, just at the threshold. The effect was limited to ispaghula, that is, psyllium husk, at 0.78 (0.63 to 0.96).

Gibb 2015: blood sugar

The analysis of 35 randomized trials found, in type 2 diabetes, a reduction in fasting glucose of 37.0 mg/dl and in HbA1c of 0.97 percentage points when psyllium was taken before meals. With normal blood sugar there was no effect, in prediabetes a moderate one. The authors worked mostly for a manufacturer of psyllium products and also used its study archive.

Where the data stop

  • Weight loss. A meta-analysis of six trials with 354 overweight participants found on average 2.1 kg less weight, prepared by employees of a manufacturer (Gibb 2023). A second meta-analysis of 27 trials found in 2025 no reduction in BMI and waist circumference and, for body weight, even an increase (Gholami 2025). Neither direction is reliable.
  • “Natural Ozempic”. A review of 52 studies on fiber and the body’s own satiety hormone GLP-1 found mostly short studies with a median of 19 participants and no consistent effect on GLP-1 and satiety at the same time (de Jong 2026). A comparison with semaglutide is not covered by these data.
  • Heart attack and stroke. The cholesterol effect is measured on a surrogate marker. Randomized trials with hard cardiovascular endpoints are not available for psyllium husk; the calculation by Zhu 2024 that the LDL reduction corresponds to about 7 percent fewer cardiovascular events is an estimate.
  • Blood sugar in healthy people. Without elevated blood sugar, Gibb 2015 found no reduction.

Status, approval and legal

In the EU, psyllium husk is a food and food supplement as well as the active ingredient of herbal medicines. The Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products of the European Medicines Agency (HMPC) has classified it in its monograph as “well-established use”: in habitual constipation, when a soft stool is meant to ease defecation (for example with anal fissures, hemorrhoids or after rectal surgery), in irritable bowel syndrome with constipation and as an adjunct to diet in elevated cholesterol. For the cholesterol use, the monograph requires medical supervision.

As a medicine, the monograph states for adolescents and adults 7 to 11 g a day for constipation and 7 to 20 g for elevated cholesterol, each in one to three doses with plenty of fluid. If constipation lasts longer than three days, a doctor or pharmacist should be consulted. In the US, the FDA allows foods containing psyllium husk a health claim on a reduced risk of coronary heart disease, based on 7 g of soluble fiber from psyllium husk per day as part of a diet low in saturated fat and cholesterol.

Safety

The most important risk is mechanical: with too little fluid, the gel can obstruct the esophagus or the bowel, up to and including choking. The HMPC gives 30 ml of water per gram as a guide and advises against taking it immediately before going to bed. Psyllium husk is not suitable in swallowing difficulties, narrowing in the gastrointestinal tract, bowel obstruction, unexplained rectal bleeding and sudden changes in bowel habits lasting more than two weeks. Older and frail people should take it only under medical supervision.

Psyllium contains strong allergens. Rhinitis, conjunctivitis, bronchospasm and, in individual cases, anaphylaxis have been described; inhaling the powder can cause sensitization, especially with frequent occupational contact. The gel delays the absorption of other drugs, including minerals, vitamin B12, cardiac glycosides, coumarins, carbamazepine and lithium, which is why the monograph provides for an interval of half an hour to one hour. With diabetes medication and thyroid hormones, a dose adjustment may be necessary; with opioids, medical supervision is provided for. Bloating is common and usually subsides over time. In the BK-Score, safety data stands at 8 out of 10: well studied, not harmless. This text is information and does not replace medical advice.

Frequently asked questions about psyllium husk

How does psyllium husk work?

Psyllium husk consists mostly of soluble fiber that swells with water into a gel. The gel holds water in the stool, makes it softer and bulkier and so stimulates bowel movement. According to the HMPC, the effect on the stool sets in after 12 to 24 hours. How exactly the gel lowers cholesterol is, in the HMPC’s assessment, not fully clarified.

Does psyllium husk lower cholesterol?

Yes, that is well supported. A meta-analysis of 28 randomized trials with 1,924 participants found, at a median of around 10.2 g per day, a reduction in LDL cholesterol of 0.33 mmol/l and in ApoB of 0.05 g/l. Studies with heart attack or stroke as an endpoint, by contrast, do not exist. The HMPC provides for use in elevated cholesterol under medical supervision.

Does psyllium husk help with weight loss?

The evidence is thin and contradictory. A meta-analysis of six small trials with 354 participants found on average 2.1 kg less weight; its authors worked for a manufacturer. A second meta-analysis of 27 trials found in 2025 no reduction in BMI and waist circumference. For a comparison with weight-loss injections such as semaglutide, there are no data.

How much water do you need with psyllium husk?

The HMPC monograph gives 30 ml of water per gram as a guide and requires that enough is also drunk over the day. With too little fluid, psyllium husk can obstruct the esophagus or the bowel. According to the HMPC, it is not suitable in swallowing difficulties or narrowing in the gastrointestinal tract, and it should not be taken immediately before going to bed.

Can psyllium husk affect how medicines work?

Yes. The gel can delay the absorption of other drugs, according to the HMPC for example of minerals, vitamin B12, cardiac glycosides, coumarins, carbamazepine and lithium. The monograph therefore provides for an interval of half an hour to one hour from other medicines. With diabetes medication and thyroid hormones, a medical dose adjustment may be necessary.

Is psyllium husk a medicine or a food?

Both. Psyllium husk is sold as a food and food supplement and is at the same time the active ingredient of herbal medicines. The EMA’s herbal committee classifies it as a medicine with well-established use, in constipation, irritable bowel syndrome with constipation and as an adjunct to diet in elevated cholesterol.
