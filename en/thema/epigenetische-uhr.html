Epigenetic age test (biological age)

Biohacking

A little saliva or a few drops of blood, then a number: your biological age. Epigenetic age tests read methylation patterns on DNA and use so-called epigenetic clocks to convert them into years or into a pace of aging. In aging research, these clocks have been a central tool for a good ten years. As a personal measurement, they have limits worth knowing before you buy.

In short

Newer clocks such as PhenoAge, GrimAge and DunedinPACE predict disease, functional limitations and mortality in large population studies, including mortality over four years in a representative sample of 3,581 older Americans. And they respond to lifestyle: in the randomized CALERIE trial, two years of caloric restriction slowed the measured pace of aging by 2 to 3 percent. For the individual, however, the number is fuzzy. Classic clocks deviated by up to 9 years on repeat measurements of the same sample, saliva and blood do not give the same result, and demographics, income, mental health and behavior predicted the further course at least as well. Whether decisions based on a test result lead to better health has not been studied. The test is a self-pay service.

What the test is and what it promises

Small chemical tags, methyl groups, sit at certain sites on the DNA. Their pattern shifts with age in a characteristic way. Epigenetic clocks are machine-learning models that estimate a number from this pattern. Which number depends on what the clock was trained on: the first generation, such as the Horvath and Hannum clocks, on chronological age, the next, such as PhenoAge and GrimAge, on health markers and time to death, DunedinPACE on the pace at which organ functions decline over the years (Apsley 2025). How epigenetics works in principle and how prevention services use it is covered under Prevention, Epigenetics & Genetics; genetic tests, by contrast, examine the unchangeable gene sequence.

The tests are offered online, with a saliva sample sent in by post, or through practices with a blood draw. Providers use different algorithms and measure different methylation sites (Apotheken Umschau 2023). The promise is understandable: a tangible number for where you stand, and a second measurement that shows whether your changes in diet, exercise or sleep have had an effect. Programs that sell exactly this before-and-after comparison are described on the page Epigenetic coaching.

What is well supported

The newer clocks are genuine risk markers. In a representative sample of older Americans, PhenoAge, GrimAge and DunedinPACE consistently predicted cognitive impairment, functional limitations, chronic disease two years later and mortality over four years (Faul 2023). That makes them valuable for research: they show how life circumstances are reflected biologically before diseases appear.

It is also established that the clocks respond to change. In CALERIE, a randomized trial, two years of caloric restriction slowed the pace of aging measured with DunedinPACE (Waziry 2023). And technical measurement noise can be reduced considerably: with methods based on principal components, most repeat measurements fell within 1.5 years of each other (Higgins-Chen 2022).

What the studies show

Faul 2023 – prediction in a representative sample

Analysis of the Health and Retirement Study with 3,581 older Americans. Age acceleration by PhenoAge, GrimAge and DunedinPACE was consistently a significant predictor of cognitive impairment, functional limitations and chronic disease two years later and of mortality over four years. Lower-noise versions based on principal components did not substantially change these associations. The authors consider the value as a predictor clear, but emphasize: demographics, socioeconomic status, mental health and health behaviors predicted later outcomes at least as well.

Higgins-Chen 2022 – technical measurement noise

When the same sample was measured several times, six widely used clocks deviated from one another by up to 9 years from technical noise alone. The research group retrained the clocks on principal components of the methylation data. These versions agreed within 1.5 years for most replicates, detected intervention effects better and produced stable trajectories.

Sehgal 2026 – technical versus biological reliability

The study examined 18 methylation clocks. Technically, most were highly reproducible, but some reacted to laboratory factors such as the position on the measurement chip or the DNA extraction method; PCGrimAge and SystemsAge remained robust. Biological reliability was markedly weaker: on repeat measurements at short intervals, under influences such as meals, stress and environmental exposures, most clocks were only poorly to moderately stable. Less reliable clocks produced variable or misleading results in interventions.

Zarandooz and Raffington 2025 – saliva versus blood

Three datasets with saliva and blood samples from the same 107 people aged 5 to 74. After correcting for cell composition, newer measures in saliva agreed moderately with the blood value: PCGrimAge with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.76, PCPhenoAge 0.72, DunedinPACE 0.68. For age acceleration by first-generation clocks, agreement was only 0.19 to 0.25. This may be enough for some research questions, the authors write, but possibly not for clinical or commercial applications.

Bruellman 2026 – saliva and blood in young adults

91 adults, 31 years old on average, gave saliva and two types of blood samples. Across 15 clocks, methylation age in saliva was 3.77 to 19.72 years higher than in blood, while the two blood fractions were comparable. One exception was DunedinPACE, which came out similar in all sample types. According to these data, saliva is not a direct substitute for blood, and comparisons only make sense within the same sample type.

CALERIE 2023 – caloric restriction as an endpoint study

220 adults without obesity were randomized for two years to 25 percent caloric restriction or a normal diet; the methylation clocks were analyzed retrospectively. DunedinPACE showed a 2 to 3 percent slower pace of aging, while PhenoAge and GrimAge did not change significantly. The authors see this as support for the idea that aging can be influenced biologically, and call for trials with disease and mortality as endpoints for definitive proof (Waziry 2023). Background on fasting and calorie reduction is available under Fasting & autophagy.

TRIIM 2019 – small study without a control group

Nine healthy men aged 51 to 65 received a combination of growth hormone, DHEA and metformin for one year, originally with the aim of regenerating the thymus. According to four clocks, epigenetic age after one year was on average about 1.5 years below baseline, or by calculation 2.5 years below the value expected without treatment; by GrimAge, a gap of 2 years persisted six months after the end of treatment (Fahy 2019). The study is often cited as evidence of “rejuvenation”. With nine participants and no control group, it is a hint, not proof, and the active substances are prescription drugs with their own risks, see Growth hormone, DHEA and Metformin. Further intervention studies, for example with omega-3, are presented on the Epigenetic coaching page.

What users report

In the European iHelp study, 178 adults from a charitable community testing program received a personal report with epigenetic age, genetic cancer risk scores and lifestyle data. Of the 91 who answered a survey after one year, 72.5 percent said they felt healthier, 60.4 percent were more physically active, 47.3 percent had improved their diet and 63.7 percent had learned more about health (Yang 2026). These are self-reports without a control group, answered by just over half of the participants, not proof of efficacy. A side finding of the same study is notable: 14 clocks compared gave clearly different results for the same samples.

Where the data stop

The strength of the clocks lies in the average across many people. For the individual, the uncertainties add up: technical noise, day-to-day fluctuations, differences between saliva and blood, between clocks and between laboratories. A change of one or two years after a program can hardly be separated from this. A 2025 review concludes that epigenetic clocks do not meet common standards for clinical utility compared with established biomarkers, and that individual-level decisions can be uninformative and potentially harmful (Apsley 2025).

Above all, the decisive proof is missing: no study has tested whether people who take such a test and act on it stay healthier or live longer than people without a test. Nor has it been shown that a lowered clock value prevents disease; the CALERIE authors themselves call for long trials with hard endpoints. And because income, mental health and behavior predict the course at least as well (Faul 2023), the number often provides less new information than its price suggests.

Status, approval and legal

Epigenetic age tests are offered in Germany as a self-pay service, online or through practices; according to a report in Apotheken Umschau, prices start at around 200 euros, with no fixed upper limit. There is no guideline that recommends the test for healthy people, and experts in aging research view the commercial offerings critically. The statutory health check-up, which is covered by German statutory health insurance, is described under Prevention, Epigenetics & Genetics.

The sample contains your DNA. Before sending it in, ask which laboratory analyzes it, how long the sample and raw data are stored and whether they are passed on to third parties.

Safety

Physically, the test is low-risk; it only needs a saliva or blood sample. The risks lie in the interpretation: an unfavorable value can cause unnecessary worry, a favorable one can falsely reassure and tempt people to neglect proven prevention such as blood pressure and cholesterol checks or cancer screening. Caution is also warranted when a single result leads directly to a supplement package.

Anyone who wants to test should ask beforehand which clock is used, whether saliva or blood is analyzed and how large the measurement uncertainty is. Before-and-after values are only comparable in the same laboratory, with the same clock and the same sample material.

Frequently asked questions about the epigenetic age test

How accurate is an epigenetic age test?

Good for groups, limited for the individual. With widely used clocks, repeat measurements of the same sample deviated by up to 9 years; newer computational methods bring most replicates within 1.5 years of each other. On top of this come fluctuations from meals, stress and the environment, which were noticeable for most clocks even at short intervals.

Does it make a difference whether saliva or blood is analyzed?

Yes. Most clocks were developed for blood. In saliva, newer measures agreed only moderately with the blood value and older ones hardly at all, and in a study of young adults, methylation age in saliva was 3.77 to 19.72 years higher depending on the clock. Comparisons only make sense within the same sample type.

Which epigenetic clock is the most meaningful?

For health and mortality, the newer clocks such as PhenoAge, GrimAge and DunedinPACE are better studied than the first generation, which was trained on chronological age. Principal-component versions such as PCGrimAge fluctuate less technically. No clock is validated for a personal decision.

Can you lower your biological age?

The measured value, in studies, yes, to a small extent. In the randomized CALERIE trial, two years of caloric restriction slowed the pace of aging by DunedinPACE by 2 to 3 percent; other clocks did not move significantly. Whether a lowered value prevents disease or extends life has not been shown.

What does an epigenetic age test cost, and does health insurance pay?

The test is a self-pay service, according to media reports from around 200 euros upward. No guideline recommends it for healthy people.

Is a repeat measurement after a few months worthwhile?

Only with caution. A change of one or two years lies within the range that measurement noise and daily fluctuations alone can produce. If at all, then in the same laboratory, with the same clock and the same sample material.
