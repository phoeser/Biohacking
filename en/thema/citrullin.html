L-citrulline (malate)

Amino acid · citrulline malate

L-citrulline is an amino acid that your body converts into arginine – and thus into the raw material for nitric oxide, which widens the blood vessels. In sport it is one of the few pump substances for which meta-analyses exist. The effect is real, but small.

In short

Citrulline raises blood arginine levels more reliably than arginine itself; this has been measured in humans. In training it brings a small advantage on average: in one meta-analysis 6.4% more repetitions to exhaustion, in the latest analysis of 30 studies an overall effect of g = 0.16. The authors themselves rate the certainty of this evidence as low to very low. For less muscle soreness after 24 hours and a slight drop in blood pressure there are signals, but no consistent data. It is well tolerated; the catch is the size of the effect, not safety.

What it is

L-citrulline is a non-essential amino acid. Your body makes it itself, and the kidney converts it into arginine. In the diet it comes, among other sources, from watermelon.

Two forms are sold: pure L-citrulline and citrulline malate, a compound with malic acid. Most sports studies have used citrulline malate, often 8 g before training. Whether the malate form contributes anything was long an open question. A direct comparison in 33 strength-trained men over 6 weeks found no difference between 8 g of pure citrulline and 12 g of citrulline malate.

Citrulline is not purely a scene product. In France, citrulline malate has been approved as a medicine since 1991, for the supportive treatment of fatigue.

How it is supposed to work

Arginine is the direct raw material for nitric oxide, NO for short. NO relaxes the vascular muscles, and blood flows more easily. The problem: swallowed arginine is largely broken down in the gut and liver before it arrives.

Citrulline bypasses this bottleneck. It is well absorbed and only converted into arginine afterwards. A double-blind study with 20 volunteers showed that citrulline raises arginine levels dose-dependently more than arginine itself and increases NO-dependent signals. This part of the chain of action is established in humans.

The next step is less clear. More arginine in the blood does not automatically mean more blood flow in the muscle. At very high single doses, arginine also rose less than expected, probably because conversion in the kidney reaches a limit. Exactly how the higher arginine level turns into the training effect has not been fully measured in humans.

How citrulline is used in training

The studies on which the meta-analyses are based mostly give citrulline malate once, 40 to 60 minutes before training, usually 6 to 8 g. What is then mostly measured is how many repetitions you manage at a fixed weight until exhaustion.

Anyone hoping for a visible pump should adjust their expectations. In the only study that asked directly about the pump sensation, in 18 trained men and women, citrulline was not better than placebo.

Besides the single dose, there are studies with daily intake over several weeks. In the largest meta-analysis, the acute dose had a more consistent effect. In a 6-week study with 33 strength-trained men, however, L-citrulline taken daily improved upper-body strength endurance compared with placebo; for strength values and the lower body there was no significant difference.

What is well supported

The mechanism is best supported: citrulline reliably raises plasma arginine, even at high single doses. A small but repeatedly found performance effect builds on this. A meta-analysis of 8 double-blind studies with 137 participants found 6.4% more repetitions to muscle failure when 6 to 8 g of citrulline malate were taken 40 to 60 minutes beforehand. The most recent and largest analysis, from 2026, pools 30 randomized studies with 644 participants and arrives at a small overall effect of g = 0.16, which is statistically significant. The single dose before training had the most consistent effect.

For recovery there is a second finding: a meta-analysis of 13 studies with 206 participants found less muscle soreness 24 hours after training. Tolerability is well documented; single doses up to 15 g caused no side effects in a dose study.

What the studies show

Meta-analysis 2026: 30 studies, small effect

Wang and colleagues analyzed 30 randomized studies with 644 participants and 138 effect sizes, literature up to August 2025. Citrulline malate slightly improved overall exercise performance (g = 0.16, p = 0.01). Perceived exertion did not change significantly. The authors emphasize wide prediction intervals and rate the evidence as low to very low according to GRADE.

Meta-analysis 2021: repetitions in strength training

Vårvik and colleagues pooled 8 double-blind, placebo-controlled studies with 137 mostly trained participants. 6 to 8 g of citrulline malate 40 to 60 minutes beforehand produced on average 3 repetitions or 6.4% more to failure (p = 0.022). For the lower body the figure was 8.1% and narrowly missed significance; for the upper body it was 5.7% without significance.

Pharmacology 2008: citrulline beats arginine

In a double-blind crossover study, 20 healthy volunteers received 6 different dosing regimens of placebo, citrulline and arginine, each for one week. Citrulline raised arginine levels dose-dependently more than arginine itself and increased NO-dependent signals. This study is the basis for the statement that the detour via citrulline works better.

Meta-analysis 2020: muscle soreness and exertion

Rhim and colleagues analyzed 13 studies with 206 participants, mostly with 8 g of citrulline malate. Muscle soreness was lower 24 hours after training (p = 0.04); after 48 and 72 hours the difference was no longer significant. Perceived exertion fell; blood lactate did not.

Where the data stop

The performance effect is small and uncertain. The authors of the largest meta-analysis note that neither dose nor timing nor target group is established. On perceived exertion, two meta-analyses contradict each other. And the pump sensation, for which citrulline is most often bought, was not better than placebo in the only study that measured it. An immediate widening of the blood vessels could not be measured at 6 and 12 g in a study with 12 men either, even though arginine levels rose markedly.

On blood pressure, caution with numbers is warranted. A 2019 meta-analysis that had found minus 7.54 mmHg was retracted. The remaining analyses are inconsistent: one found minus 4.10 mmHg systolic, another no effect. On erections there is only one small, single-blind study with 24 men, in which half reported erections one grade harder with citrulline, compared with 8.3% with placebo. That is a signal, not proof. Studies beyond 17 weeks are missing.

Status, approval and legal

In Germany, citrulline is sold as a dietary supplement. For an arginine-citrulline product, the Federal Office of Consumer Protection and Food Safety issued a general ruling in 2013, with at most 65 mg of citrulline per day and warning notices. In France, citrulline malate has been approved as a medicine since 1991, 1 g per sachet. For competitive athletes, the general advice of the National Anti Doping Agency applies: dietary supplements can be contaminated, and the Cologne List lists independently tested products.

Safety

Citrulline is well tolerated. In a dose study with 8 men, single doses up to 15 g caused no side effects. In a training study with 41 men, 14.63% complained of stomach discomfort after 8 g of citrulline malate; the French summary of product characteristics lists occasional mild heartburn. Because citrulline intervenes in NO metabolism, the German general ruling provides for consulting a doctor if you take blood pressure medication or have kidney or liver disease. It also prescribes a warning notice for children, pregnant and breastfeeding women and people after a heart attack. There is no official upper limit, and the controlled studies ran for at most 17 weeks.

Frequently asked questions about L-citrulline (malate)

How much citrulline was taken in the studies?

The strength studies mostly used 6 to 8 g of citrulline malate as a single dose 40 to 60 minutes before training. The blood pressure studies used 3 to 9 g of pure citrulline daily for 1 to 17 weeks. This is a description of the studies, not a personal recommendation.

Is citrulline malate better than pure L-citrulline?

That has not been shown. In a direct comparison in 33 trained men over 6 weeks, there was no difference between 8 g of L-citrulline and 12 g of citrulline malate. Most performance studies have nonetheless used the malate form.

Why citrulline and not arginine directly?

Swallowed arginine is largely broken down in the gut and liver. Citrulline bypasses this breakdown and is only converted to arginine afterwards. In a comparative study, citrulline raised arginine levels more than arginine itself.

Does citrulline help against muscle soreness?

A meta-analysis of 13 studies found less muscle soreness 24 hours after training. After 48 and 72 hours the difference was no longer significant. A noticeable but short-lived effect is therefore possible, reliable protection is not.

Does citrulline lower blood pressure?

Possibly, but the evidence is not consistent. One meta-analysis found minus 4.10 mmHg systolic, another found no effect, and a third analysis was retracted. Anyone taking blood pressure medication should check with a doctor before taking it.

Can I replace citrulline with watermelon?

Watermelon contains citrulline, and a meta-analysis on blood pressure pooled studies with citrulline and with watermelon. The training data, by contrast, come almost entirely from studies with citrulline powder. Whether you can reach the same amount with fruit has not been studied.
