Reishi

Mushroom · Ganoderma lucidum

Reishi, called lingzhi in China, is a large, reddish-brown, fan-shaped mushroom that has been regarded in Asia for more than 2,000 years as a remedy and tonic. There is a whole series of human studies, and they show a fairly clear pattern: indications of benefit for fatigue and quality of life, but no effect on blood sugar, blood lipids and inflammation markers.

In short

In a double-blind trial with 132 patients, reishi improved fatigue states considerably more than placebo, and in cancer patients a Cochrane review found slightly increased immune cell values and a better quality of life. For blood sugar, blood lipids and blood pressure, by contrast, two Cochrane analyses and a more recent meta-analysis show no effect; the quality of evidence is low throughout. For the often advertised better sleep, we found not a single human study. Reishi is usually well tolerated, but liver injury has rarely been described, and in Germany the authorities consider it a medicine without approval, which is why this page gives no dosage.

What it is

What is sold as reishi is usually Ganoderma lucidum; the Chinese lingzhi is sometimes classified botanically as a separate species, Ganoderma lingzhi. The dried fruiting body, extracts made from it and the spores are used. The most important constituents are polysaccharides, including beta-glucans from the cell wall, and triterpenoids, plus nucleosides.

In traditional Chinese medicine, reishi is used to promote health, restore vitality and prevent disease; in marketing it is often called the mushroom of immortality. Today it is advertised mainly for the immune system, stress, sleep and liver protection.

How it is supposed to work

The polysaccharides are supposed to stimulate immune cells, and the triterpenoids to dampen inflammation and protect the liver. These mechanisms come predominantly from laboratory research and animal experiments. What has been measured in humans is mainly immune cell counts in the blood, questionnaires on fatigue and quality of life, and metabolic values.

An example of how far apart laboratory and human results can be: test-tube experiments suggested that reishi inhibits blood platelets. In a double-blind trial with 40 healthy volunteers, however, none of the measured clotting values changed over 4 weeks.

What is well supported

The most convincing data concern fatigue. In a multicenter, double-blind trial with 132 patients with neurasthenia, a fatigue syndrome according to ICD-10, 51.6 percent of patients improved more than minimally on a reishi polysaccharide extract over 8 weeks, compared with 24.6 percent on placebo. A smaller randomized pilot study with 48 breast cancer patients found less cancer-related fatigue, less anxiety and a better quality of life after 4 weeks.

In cancer patients, a Cochrane review analyzed 5 randomized trials: with reishi, the proportions of various immune cells in the blood rose slightly, for example CD3 by 3.91 percent, and in 4 trials quality of life was better. In athletes, a meta-analysis of 6 studies on Ganoderma found lower lactate and urea values and a higher hematocrit, that is, indications of better recovery. A small study with 78 female students reported better values for endurance, anxiety and well-being after 30 days.

As a report, not as proof of efficacy, a survey of 1,374 cancer patients in China who took reishi is worth a look. About half stated that nausea (55 percent), fatigue (52 percent) and low mood (50 percent) had improved considerably. There was no control group.

What the studies show

Tang 2005, neurasthenia

Multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 132 patients, 8 weeks, 123 of them evaluable. Severity and fatigue decreased more with reishi than with placebo, fatigue by 28.3 versus 20.1 percent. 51.6 versus 24.6 percent improved more than minimally. The extract was well tolerated.

Cochrane 2016, supportive cancer care

5 randomized trials, methodologically unsatisfactory in the authors’ judgment. Tumor response in combination with chemotherapy or radiotherapy was more favorable with a risk ratio of 1.50, but the confidence interval of 0.90 to 2.51 also includes no effect. No trial recorded long-term survival. Conclusion: not a first-line therapy, at most worth considering as an add-on.

Cochrane 2015 and RCT 2016, cardiovascular

5 trials with 398 participants on blood sugar, blood pressure and blood lipids, plus the authors’ own double-blind trial with 84 people with type 2 diabetes over 16 weeks. No effect on HbA1c, fasting glucose, cholesterol, blood pressure or triglycerides. The authors see no basis for using reishi to treat these risk factors.

Kwok 2005, blood clotting

Double-blind trial with 40 healthy volunteers, 20 each with reishi or placebo over 4 weeks. Measurements included a coagulation screen, fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor, platelet function and thromboelastography, before intake and after 4 and 8 weeks. There was no difference between the groups; all values remained within the normal range. People on anticoagulants were not included.

Li 2007, rheumatoid arthritis

65 patients with active rheumatoid arthritis received reishi with a traditional herbal mixture or placebo for 24 weeks in addition to their therapy. The primary endpoint, a 20 percent improvement according to the American College of Rheumatology criteria, was reached by 15 versus 9.1 percent; the difference was not significant. Pain and patient assessment improved only in the reishi group; inflammation and immune values remained unchanged.

Meta-analysis Jafari 2025

17 randomized trials with 971 participants over 1 to 24 weeks. Small effects on body mass index, creatinine and heart rate, none on inflammation markers, liver enzymes, blood lipids or blood sugar. The authors rate the quality of evidence as very low for all endpoints.

Where the data stop

For sleep, one of the most heavily advertised effects, we found no human study, only experiments in mice. In the patient survey, insomnia even appeared as a side effect. An anti-inflammatory effect has not been demonstrated in humans either: the meta-analysis found no effect on inflammation markers, and in a trial with 65 rheumatism patients over 24 weeks, pain and patient assessment did improve, but measurable anti-inflammatory effects did not materialize. Human studies on liver protection are lacking; the meta-analysis saw no effect on liver enzymes.

For cancer, the Cochrane review sees insufficient evidence; an effect on survival has not been studied. In 2015, the joint expert commission of BVL and BfArM stated that advertising claims about cancer, aging, rheumatism or depression for medicinal mushrooms such as reishi lack a scientific basis. Most studies are small, short and use different preparations, from fruiting body powder to spores and extracts.

Status, approval and legal

According to the assessment of a joint expert commission of BVL and BfArM from 2015, products made from Ganoderma lucidum, as well as from Cordyceps sinensis and Coriolus versicolor, are to be regarded not as foods but as medicines. Reishi is not approved as a medicine in Germany. According to the Verbraucherzentrale (German consumer advice centre), the fruiting bodies have been used as dietary supplements in the EU for more than 20 years. Because it is not an approved active substance, this page gives no dosage. No relevance to doping is noted in the sources checked.

Safety

In the studies, reishi was mostly well tolerated; in the survey of the 1,374 cancer patients, 9.1 percent reported side effects, most often dry mouth, constipation, insomnia, itching and dizziness. The US database LiverTox classifies reishi as a possible rare cause of liver injury; individual cases have been described, including one fatal case, mostly after 1 to 6 months of intake. A case report describes acute hepatitis after reishi powder taken together with alcohol. According to the Verbraucherzentrale, reishi can lead to dangerous hypoglycemia, even in people without diabetes; caution is therefore advised with diabetes medication. Beta-glucan extracts from mushrooms should not be taken together with cortisone or anti-inflammatory painkillers. A study in healthy people found no effect on blood clotting, but reishi has not been studied in people taking anticoagulants; this should be clarified with a doctor beforehand. There are no data for pregnant and breastfeeding women.

Frequently asked questions about reishi

Does reishi help with sleep?

There is no human study on this, only experiments in mice. In a large patient survey, insomnia was even named as a side effect. What is supported is rather evidence against fatigue.

Does reishi strengthen the immune system?

In cancer patients, some immune cell values in the blood rose slightly. Whether this protects against infections or influences the course of disease has not been studied.

Can reishi help against cancer?

The Cochrane review finds insufficient evidence for an effect on tumors and no data on survival. As an add-on, reishi improved quality of life in some small trials. It does not replace cancer treatment; any intake should be coordinated with the treatment team.

Does reishi lower blood sugar or cholesterol?

Not in controlled trials. Two Cochrane analyses and a meta-analysis found no effect on HbA1c, fasting glucose or blood lipids. There are nonetheless individual reports of hypoglycemia.

Is reishi harmful to the liver?

In studies, no abnormal liver values were noticed, and the meta-analysis found no influence on liver enzymes. LiverTox, however, describes rare individual cases of liver injury. In case of fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain or yellowing of the skin during intake, stop taking it and have it checked by a doctor.

Can reishi be taken with blood thinners?

In healthy people, reishi did not change blood clotting in one study. It has never been studied together with anticoagulants, however, so clarify this with a doctor beforehand.
