L-ornithine

Amino acid · ornithine, L-ornithine hydrochloride, L-ornithine L-aspartate, LOLA, ornithine aspartate

L-ornithine is an amino acid that the body needs not for protein but for detoxification: in the liver’s urea cycle it helps render ammonia harmless. As a food supplement it is taken for fatigue, stress and poor sleep; as L-ornithine L-aspartate it is an approved liver medicine in Germany. These two worlds should be kept apart.

In short

L-ornithine is an intermediate of the urea cycle, with which the liver detoxifies ammonia. In healthy people, several small placebo-controlled studies improved mainly subjective fatigue, anger and sleep quality, one of them also the cortisol level. The compound L-ornithine L-aspartate is approved in Germany as a pharmacy-only medicine for hepatic encephalopathy in liver cirrhosis; the Cochrane analysis sees a possible benefit there, with very low quality of evidence. Ornithine is well tolerated, but the studies in healthy people are small, and an ammonia effect during exercise could not be confirmed.

What it is

Ornithine is one of the amino acids that are not incorporated into protein. The body produces it itself; it occurs in foods only in small amounts, and meat and fish are not rich in it. It performs its main task in the liver: in the urea cycle, ammonia, a toxic waste product of protein metabolism, is converted into urea and excreted via the kidneys. Ornithine is both starting material and activator in this process.

As a preparation there are two forms that are legally separate. L-ornithine, usually as the hydrochloride, is sold as a food supplement. L-ornithine L-aspartate, LOLA for short, combines ornithine with the amino acid aspartate and is approved in Germany as a medicine for the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy, a brain dysfunction caused by too much ammonia in severe liver disease. The data on the medicine come almost entirely from people with liver cirrhosis and cannot simply be transferred to healthy people.

How it is supposed to work

The basic idea is simple: more ornithine is supposed to drive the urea cycle and break down ammonia faster. With a diseased liver, this makes sense. According to the summary of product characteristics, LOLA acts on two pathways of ammonia detoxification, urea synthesis and glutamine synthesis. The same summary of product characteristics, however, states that under normal conditions ornithine and aspartate are not the limiting factor for urea synthesis. So with a healthy liver, the raw material is usually not what is missing.

For athletes, the hope behind it is that less ammonia during hard exertion means less fatigue. For everyday life, ornithine is advertised as a remedy for stress and for better sleep, because it dampened the stress response in animal experiments. Older promises from the bodybuilding scene revolve around the release of growth hormone.

What users are looking for

Many reach for ornithine when they find it hard to switch off in the evening, do not feel rested in the morning, or want to do something for their liver after a long night. Athletes hope for less exhaustion. The studies measure exactly such subjective improvements, and these vary from person to person. Anyone who tries it is best off keeping a simple sleep and fatigue diary for a few weeks.

What is well supported

The data are strongest for the medicine LOLA. The Cochrane analysis found 36 randomized trials in liver cirrhosis; data could be used from 29 with 1,891 participants. Compared with placebo or no treatment, hepatic encephalopathy occurred less often, with a relative risk of 0.70 across 22 trials, and mortality was lower, at 0.42 across 19 trials. In Germany, LOLA is approved as Hepa-Merz, pharmacy-only, for the treatment of latent and manifest hepatic encephalopathy.

In healthy people there are several small but properly blinded studies. 52 working adults with mild stress took 400 mg of ornithine per day for 8 weeks: cortisol fell, anger declined, and sleep quality was rated as better. In a study with 65 healthy people, 1,600 mg over 7 days improved fatigue and anger on the morning after a social stress test. And in 17 volunteers, ornithine eased perceived exhaustion after two hours of exercise on an ergometer. Add to this good tolerability: a safety review of 22 studies estimates the NOAEL at 12 g per day, and a tolerability study with 60 men found no treatment-related side effects up to this amount over 4 weeks.

What the studies show

Cochrane review on LOLA in liver cirrhosis, 2018

The authors analysed data from 29 randomized trials with 1,891 people with cirrhosis. Calculated across all trials, LOLA lowered the risk of encephalopathy and death. In the few trials with low risk of bias, the effect disappeared, and the quality of the evidence was rated as very low. Compared with lactulose and rifaximin, the standard agents, no difference was found.

Ornithine for stress and sleep

52 healthy Japanese working adults who felt mildly stressed and tired took 400 mg of L-ornithine per day or placebo for 8 weeks. In the ornithine group, serum cortisol and the ratio of cortisol to DHEA-S fell, anger declined, and participants rated their sleep as better. Sleep quality was recorded by questionnaire, not in a sleep laboratory.

Ornithine in exercise without an ammonia effect

In a crossover study, 16 young men took tea catechins with ornithine or placebo, once and over 14 days. Neither the ammonia level during 60 minutes of cycling nor performance in the subsequent 15-minute test differed. The exertion, however, felt less strenuous with the preparation.

Where the data stop

The studies in healthy people are small, with 17 to 65 participants, come predominantly from Japan and mainly measure subjective variables such as fatigue, mood and perceived sleep. On cortisol they contradict each other: in the eight-week study it fell, in the stress test study it remained unchanged. The hope that ornithine lowers ammonia during exercise was not confirmed in a controlled study, and according to the authors a performance advantage in maximal sprints could not be explained by ammonia.

The growth hormone narrative rests on a study with 17 athletes who took ornithine together with arginine. That ornithine releases insulin was not confirmed in 12 bodybuilders. For the medicine LOLA, the benefit remains uncertain despite many trials, because the effect was not seen in the methodologically best trials. For fatty liver, there are first positive liver values from 3 randomized trials; large randomized trials are lacking. For ornithine as a remedy after alcohol, there are no reliable human data.

Status, approval and legal

In Germany, ornithine has two separate roles. As a food supplement, the BVL (Federal Office of Consumer Protection and Food Safety) permitted capsules with L-ornithine from other EU states by general ruling in 2014, with a maximum of 500 mg per capsule at 2 capsules daily. A separate general ruling for L-ornithine L-aspartate as a food supplement is not known. As a medicine, L-ornithine L-aspartate is approved, pharmacy-only, under the name Hepa-Merz for the treatment of latent and manifest hepatic encephalopathy, with up to 1 to 2 sachets of 3.0 g three times daily. Food supplements may not be advertised for the treatment of liver diseases. Ornithine is not on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List.

Safety

Ornithine is well tolerated. In 22 studies with healthy people, lasting up to 156 days, gastrointestinal complaints were the most important side effect, and the risk did not differ from placebo. The summary of product characteristics of the medicine lists occasional nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, flatulence and diarrhoea, and very rarely limb pain. LOLA is contraindicated in more severe kidney dysfunction, should be avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and there are no data for children. The granules contain fructose and are unsuitable in hereditary fructose intolerance. Anyone with liver or kidney disease should therefore clarify intake with a physician instead of self-treating. In the rare hereditary disease gyrate atrophy, ornithine levels are already greatly elevated; additional ornithine is not something to take on one’s own there.

Frequently asked questions about L-ornithine

Does L-ornithine help with sleep?

In a placebo-controlled study with 52 stressed working adults, participants rated their sleep as better after 8 weeks with ornithine. It was measured by questionnaire, not in a sleep laboratory. So far there are hardly any other sleep studies.

What is the difference between L-ornithine and L-ornithine L-aspartate?

L-ornithine is the single amino acid and is sold in Germany as a food supplement. L-ornithine L-aspartate combines ornithine with aspartate and is approved as a pharmacy-only medicine for the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy in liver cirrhosis. The studies on the medicine concern almost exclusively people with liver disease.

Does L-ornithine detoxify the liver?

In severe liver disease, LOLA may help break down ammonia, but the evidence is of very low quality. With a healthy liver, according to the summary of product characteristics, ornithine is not the limiting factor in urea formation. A general detoxification for healthy people is not proven.

Does L-ornithine work against fatigue after exercise?

Several small studies found that exertion and exhaustion subjectively felt lighter with ornithine. In a controlled study, however, it did not lower the ammonia level during cycling, and performance stayed the same. So it acts more on perception than measurably on performance.

Does L-ornithine increase growth hormone?

This is supported only by one small study with 17 strength athletes who took ornithine together with arginine. There are no reliable data on this for ornithine alone. Insulin release was not found in bodybuilders.

Is L-ornithine safe?

A review of 22 studies found mainly gastrointestinal complaints and estimated the NOAEL at 12 g per day. The medicine is contraindicated in more severe kidney dysfunction and should be avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding. In liver or kidney disease, intake belongs in the hands of a physician.
