GABA

Amino acid · gamma-aminobutyric acid

GABA is the most important calming messenger in the brain and has always been present in fermented foods such as kimchi. As a capsule, it is taken for relaxation and sleep. The studies show measurable effects on blood pressure, stress markers and growth hormone, but the central question remains open: how does the effect come about?

In short

Oral GABA is rapidly absorbed into the blood and is well tolerated in the short term. A systematic review of 14 placebo-controlled studies found limited evidence of less stress and very limited evidence of better sleep. In addition, there are small studies in which GABA lowered systolic blood pressure and raised growth hormone. Many users report noticeable relaxation. The catch: whether GABA from a capsule reaches the brain at all is unresolved, and almost all studies are small and often close to industry.

What it is

Gamma-aminobutyric acid is an amino acid that the body does not incorporate into proteins but uses as a signaling substance. In the brain, it inhibits the excitation of nerve cells. Many sedatives and sleeping pills act precisely at its receptors.

GABA occurs in foods, especially fermented ones: kimchi, green tea and lactic-acid-fermented products contain appreciable amounts. Dietary supplements usually contain isolated GABA obtained by fermentation. Some of the studies, by contrast, worked with enriched foods such as fermented milk or chlorella, which contain only a few milligrams.

How it is supposed to work

The obvious explanation is: swallow GABA, raise GABA in the brain, become calmer. It is not that simple. GABA enters the blood quickly: in a study with 2 grams, the blood level peaked after 0.5 to 1 hour, and the half-life was 5 hours. Whether it then crosses the blood-brain barrier in relevant amounts is something the available studies show contradictorily. The BfR (German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment) also considers this unresolved.

There is a second, equally plausible route: GABA receptors are also located in the gut and in its own nervous system, which is connected to the brain via the vagus nerve. A review in Frontiers in Psychology considers both routes possible, direct passage and the detour via the gut. In favor of this is that many studies found effects mainly in the autonomic nervous system, for example in heart rate variability. Neither route has been proven.

What users report

GABA is one of the supplements for which many users describe a noticeable effect. As early as 2015, Boonstra and colleagues noted that numerous consumers report effects, and asked whether these go beyond a placebo effect. One note that many US products carry on the label is revealing: shortly after intake, a slight tingling of the skin or slight shortness of breath may occur, which quickly subsides. This is a manufacturer statement, but it fits with studies using high doses in which participants briefly felt a burning sensation in the throat and shortness of breath.

Such reports say: something is happening, and quickly. They do not say that the calming effect runs through the brain. The tingling in particular points rather to an effect in the body itself.

What is well supported

The best-supported effects are small but measurable physical ones. For blood pressure, GABA-rich chlorella with 20 milligrams of GABA twice daily over 12 weeks lowered the systolic value significantly more than placebo in 80 people with high-normal blood pressure. A fermented milk with GABA produced similar results in 39 mildly hypertensive patients. The US Pharmacopeia’s safety assessment summarizes that blood pressure fell temporarily and moderately in some studies, by less than 10 percent. For stress, the review of 14 studies found positive signals mainly in physical markers such as heart rate variability, cortisol and salivary immunoglobulin. And for growth hormone, a small, well-controlled study showed a marked acute rise.

What the studies show

Review of 14 placebo-controlled studies

In 2020, Hepsomali and colleagues included only placebo-controlled human studies with natural or fermentation-derived GABA. After a single dose, most studies found no subjective improvement in stress, but did find changes in stress markers. For sleep, subjective scores improved only when GABA was taken for at least one week. The conclusion: limited evidence for stress, very limited for sleep. In 11 of the 14 studies, at least one author was employed by a company.

Sleep in the sleep lab

Byun and colleagues gave 40 people with difficulty falling asleep 300 milligrams of GABA from rice germ or placebo for 4 weeks, in a ratio of 30 to 10. Measurement was objective, by polysomnography. In the GABA group, the time to fall asleep fell from 13.4 to 5.7 minutes, and sleep efficiency rose from 79.4 to 86.1 percent. The changes were compared within the groups, and with 10 people, the placebo group was very small.

Growth hormone after 3 grams

Powers and colleagues gave 11 strength-trained men 3 grams of GABA or placebo in a crossover design. At rest, peak growth hormone levels were about 400 percent higher, and after training 30 minutes after intake, 200 percent higher. The authors left open whether this builds muscle. A later study in 21 men found a greater gain in fat-free mass after 12 weeks of strength training with whey protein plus 100 milligrams of GABA than with whey protein alone.

Where the data stop

The core question has not been answered: no one has yet directly shown that oral GABA increases the amount of GABA in the human brain. As long as that is missing, it remains open whether the calming effects arise via the brain, via the gut or via expectation. Then there is the quality of the studies. They are consistently small, use very different products and doses, from a few milligrams in fermented milk to 3 grams of pure substance, and are frequently close to industry. For sleep, larger studies with objective measurement and a fair placebo comparison are lacking. The popular combination with L-theanine has only been studied in humans in a single-arm study with 19 participants without a placebo group; the PSQI sleep index improved there from 9.42 to 6.26, but without a control group this cannot be separated from a placebo effect. There are hardly any long-term data over months; a recent review from 2026 states that human studies have not yet demonstrated clear clinical efficacy.

Status, approval and legal

According to the Bavarian State Office for Health and Food Safety, GABA can currently be used in dietary supplements in Germany without special authorization, but healing claims are not permitted. There is no legal maximum amount, and no authorized health claim either. According to Apotheken Umschau, the BfR currently sees no sufficiently reliable data for a health assessment. In the USA, GABA is a dietary supplement; two applications for status as generally recognized as safe were withdrawn by the applicant in 2008 and 2015. GABA is not on the WADA prohibited list.

Safety

In the short term, GABA is well studied and tolerated. The US Pharmacopeia’s safety assessment found no serious side effects up to 18 grams per day over 4 days and at 120 milligrams per day over 12 weeks, and neither case reports nor spontaneous reports. Mild complaints were reported: abdominal discomfort, headache, drowsiness and, at high doses, a brief burning sensation in the throat, sometimes with shortness of breath. Because GABA can lower blood pressure, the assessment advises caution when blood pressure medication is taken at the same time. GABA can raise growth hormone and prolactin; there are no studies on pregnancy and breastfeeding, where restraint is advisable. In its monograph, the Canadian health authority states a maximum of 750 milligrams per single dose and advises consulting a health professional from 300 milligrams daily when taken for more than 4 weeks. Anyone taking sleeping pills or sedatives should discuss intake with a doctor beforehand.

Frequently asked questions about GABA

Does GABA from a capsule reach the brain?

That is unresolved. GABA does enter the blood quickly, but the studies on the blood-brain barrier contradict each other, and a direct measurement in the human brain after intake is lacking. A route via the gut’s nervous system is also being discussed.

Does GABA help you fall asleep?

The evidence is weak. A review of 14 studies rates the evidence for sleep as very limited. In a small study with measurement in the sleep lab, the time to fall asleep decreased after 4 weeks with 300 milligrams daily, but the placebo group was very small.

Why does it tingle after intake?

Many US products state on the label that slight skin tingling or brief shortness of breath may occur shortly after intake. In studies with several grams, participants reported a brief burning sensation in the throat. The complaints subsided quickly in each case; their mechanism is not clear.

How quickly does GABA work?

GABA peaks in the blood after 0.5 to 1 hour, and the half-life is about 5 hours. In an EEG study, changes appeared 60 minutes after intake. For sleep, subjective scores in studies improved only after at least one week of regular intake.

Does GABA lower blood pressure?

In two small studies, systolic blood pressure fell more than with placebo in people with slightly elevated values. The effects were moderate. Anyone taking blood pressure medication should only add GABA after consulting a doctor.

Can GABA be combined with L-theanine?

The combination is widespread but barely studied. In humans, there is only one small study without a placebo group, in which sleep quality improved. Whether the combination does more than either substance alone is open.
