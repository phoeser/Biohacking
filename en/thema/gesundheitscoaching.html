Treatment & Procedure

Health coaching

Focus

Most people know what is healthy. Putting it into practice in everyday life is the real problem. Health coaching starts exactly there: a trained person helps you set your own goals and stick with them.

In short

Health coaching is structured support for behavior change, for example around exercise, diet, stress or sleep. Best supported are small effects: more physical activity, somewhat less pain and disability in chronic pain, and lower blood pressure in hypertension. For weight loss, the effect is barely measurable in high-quality studies. The catch: coaching is not a protected term, quality varies widely, and a coach is not permitted to treat illnesses.

What lies behind the method

Good health coaching does not give instructions; it works with questions. The person sets their own goals, breaks them down into small steps, monitors their own progress and talks through setbacks. The techniques come from behavioral research: shared goal setting, motivational interviewing, self-monitoring and regular feedback.

This can take place in person, by phone, by video or in an app with human support. Those who offer it vary greatly: nurses, physicians, psychologists, sports scientists, nutrition professionals, but also people with a weekend course.

How to recognize a reputable offer

A good offer begins with a conversation about goals and clarifies whether a medical examination is needed first. It works with verifiable interim goals instead of promises, does not sell dietary supplements on the side, and refers people to physicians or psychotherapists when there are signs of depression, an anxiety disorder or severe exhaustion. It is worth asking about the training, the method and how success is measured.

What is well supported

The most robust data exist for two areas. In chronic pain not related to cancer, a 2024 meta-analysis pools a total of 26 randomized trials with 4,403 participants: coaching improved disability and pain to a small extent and increased physical activity somewhat. In high blood pressure, coaching lowered systolic and diastolic values in 12 randomized trials with 2,497 participants; the trials mostly had a low risk of bias. Most effective there was coaching by phone and by nurses.

A 2026 review of 54 papers also shows: the more carefully coaching implements its core elements, that is, shared goal setting and strategic questions, the more often the results were positive.

What the studies show

Coaching in chronic pain, meta-analysis 2024

Barnet-Hepples and colleagues analyzed 26 randomized trials with 4,403 adults who received coaching to increase physical activity. Disability and pain improved to a small extent, activity trivially to slightly. Certainty ranged from moderate for disability to very low for pain; the effect on quality of life remained unclear.

Coaching in high blood pressure, meta-analysis 2023

Meng and colleagues pooled 12 randomized trials with 2,497 people with hypertension. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure fell significantly, and eating habits and self-efficacy also improved. The authors rated the quality of the evidence as high.

Coaching for weight loss, meta-analysis 2021

Sieczkowska and colleagues included 38 studies. Compared with usual care, the effect on weight was trivial, there were signs of publication bias, and in the high-quality studies no effect was detectable anymore.

Where the data stop

The biggest problem is the term itself. What studies call health coaching ranges from a single phone call to a program lasting months. In the 2026 review, only 24 percent of the interventions examined met the criteria for high quality. The good results therefore cannot be transferred to every offer.

For some popular goals the data are weak. For weight loss, high-quality studies showed no effect; for sleep quality and alcohol consumption, a review of middle-aged adults found no significant changes. How long behavior changes last after coaching ends has barely been studied. And according to the World Health Organization, burnout is not a medical diagnosis but an occupational phenomenon; there are no studies with hard endpoints for coaching in that area.

Status, approval and legal

Health coaching is not a regulated health profession. Anyone who diagnoses, cures or alleviates illnesses is practicing medicine under the German Non-Medical Practitioners Act (Heilpraktikergesetz) and needs a medical license or a permit to do so. A coach therefore operates in the field of health promotion. Individual coaching is generally paid for out of pocket. The statutory health insurers do, however, subsidize certified prevention courses under Section 20 SGB V, for example on exercise, nutrition or stress management. Each insurer sets the amount of the subsidy itself.

Safety

Coaching carries hardly any direct risks. The real risk is that an illness requiring treatment disappears behind the word stress. Persistent exhaustion, sleep disturbances, low mood or marked weight loss should first be checked by a physician. Anyone taking medication, for example for high blood pressure or diabetes, should discuss major changes to diet and training with their doctor.

Frequently asked questions about health coaching

What does a health coach do?

A health coach helps people set their own health goals and put them into practice in everyday life, for example more exercise, a better diet or less stress. Good coaches work with questions, small steps and regular feedback rather than ready-made plans.

Does health coaching work?

For some goals, yes, to a small extent. Supported are more physical activity, somewhat less pain and disability in chronic pain, and lower blood pressure in hypertension. For weight loss, high-quality studies show hardly any effect.

Does health insurance pay for health coaching?

Individual coaching usually not. Certified prevention courses under Section 20 SGB V on exercise, nutrition or stress management are, however, subsidized. How much is up to each insurer.

Is health coach a protected profession?

No, it is not a regulated health profession with standardized training. Quality therefore varies widely. It is worth asking about training, method and how success is measured.

Does coaching help with burnout?

According to the World Health Organization, burnout is not a medical diagnosis but an occupational phenomenon. Coaching can help with coping with strain, but there are no studies with hard endpoints for this. If depression or another illness lies behind it, it belongs in the hands of a physician or psychotherapist.

How do I recognize a reputable offer?

By verifiable goals instead of promises, by a transparent method and by referral to physicians when there are signs of illness. Promises of cures and the sale of the coach’s own products during coaching should make you skeptical.
