Peptide & experimental

Orexin A

Endogenous neuropeptide (33 amino acids), agonist at both orexin receptors · hypocretin-1 · OxA

Orexin A is the peptide whose deficiency characterizes narcolepsy type 1, and the only orexin peptide that has been tested in humans at all. Since a 2007 study showed that, given as a nasal spray, it restored memory performance in sleep-deprived monkeys, it has been seen as a candidate against sleep loss. The human data are smaller and more cautious: given intranasally, the peptide measurably does something in the brain, but in studies so far it has not made anyone more awake.

In short

Orexin A is an endogenous neuropeptide from the hypothalamus that activates both orexin receptors and stabilizes the waking state. Unlike its sister peptide orexin B, it passes intact from the blood into the brain in animal experiments. In rhesus monkeys after 30 to 36 hours without sleep, it improved performance in a memory task, including when given as a spray mist into the nose. In humans there are four small placebo-controlled studies with a single intranasal dose: in narcolepsy patients, orexin A stabilized REM sleep, improved the sense of smell and reduced errors in an attention test; in healthy men, it increased sympathetic activity. The patients did not become more awake, healthy people with sleep loss were never studied, and how much arrives in the brain via the nose is questionable according to a PET study.

What orexin A is

Orexin A and orexin B are formed from the same precursor, prepro-orexin, in nerve cells of the lateral hypothalamus. Orexin A is the larger of the two peptides, activates both orexin receptors and, after the second group that discovered it, is also called hypocretin-1. We describe the 1998 discovery and the receptors in more detail on the page on orexin B.

The system keeps the waking state stable. When the orexin cells die off, narcolepsy type 1 develops, with daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, sleep paralysis and disrupted night sleep; in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with narcolepsy with cataplexy, orexin A is greatly reduced or not detectable at all. From this deficiency comes the obvious idea of replacing the missing peptide from outside.

On the market, orexin A appears as a laboratory peptide labeled “for research purposes only”, sometimes as a nasal spray, advertised against fatigue and sleep loss. Sleep medications that block exactly these receptors take the opposite route: the orexin receptor antagonists.

How it is supposed to work

If the signal is missing, you give it back – with peptides, this usually fails because of the route into the brain. Here orexin A has a real advantage: Kastin and Akerstrom showed in mice in 1999 that labeled orexin A, after intravenous administration, passes quickly and by simple diffusion into brain tissue and arrives there largely intact. With an octanol partition coefficient of 0.232, it is considerably more fat-soluble than orexin B at 0.030, which in addition breaks down rapidly in the blood.

The human experiments rely on intranasal administration. The Kiel research group, from which three of the four human studies come, based this on earlier findings according to which neuropeptides reach the brain directly by this route. Other peptides are also used via the nose, for example Semax. How well this route works for orexin A, however, was called into question by a PET study in 2018 (see below).

What is well supported

What is supported first of all is the biology: the link between the loss of orexin cells and narcolepsy type 1, and the lack of orexin A in the cerebrospinal fluid of those affected. It is also supported that orexin A crosses the blood-brain barrier in animal experiments, unlike orexin B.

It is remarkable that intranasal orexin A has measurable effects in humans at all. Four double-blind, placebo-controlled studies with a crossover design – each participant received the peptide once and placebo once – each found a difference: in olfactory threshold, in REM sleep, in an attention test and in sympathetic activity. This suggests that at least part of the peptide acts centrally. That activating the orexin system can actually help people with narcolepsy type 1 has been demonstrated since August 2026 by the US approval of a tablet drug that occupies orexin receptor 2 directly.

What the studies show

Sleep-deprived monkeys (2007)

Deadwyler and colleagues kept rhesus monkeys awake for 30 to 36 hours and gave them orexin A before a short-term memory task, either intravenously or as a fine spray mist into the nose. Both routes improved the performance of the sleep-deprived animals, the nasal route even more than the highest intravenous dose tested. The effect appeared mainly in difficult tasks, and the glucose metabolism in the brain regions involved, altered by sleep deprivation, moved back toward normal. In rested animals the peptide did not change performance, except at the highest intravenous dose. On the question of sleep loss, it is the central study – there is no study on this in humans.

Smell in narcolepsy (2008)

Baier and colleagues found a weaker sense of smell in ten patients with narcolepsy with cataplexy than in matched healthy people. Seven of them then received orexin A or placebo intranasally in a double-blind fashion. After orexin A, the olfactory threshold rose in all patients, to a median of 11.5 versus 7.75 points under placebo. The authors see this as an indication that the orexin deficiency in the brain explains the smell disorder.

Night sleep in narcolepsy (2011)

In a pilot study, the same Kiel group gave eight patients orexin A or placebo intranasally before sleep and recorded the night in the sleep laboratory. Nocturnal wake time did not change significantly. REM sleep, however, decreased, especially in the second half of the night, and direct transitions from wakefulness into REM sleep, a typical feature of narcolepsy, became less frequent.

Wakefulness and attention during the day (2014)

Weinhold and colleagues repeated the approach with 14 patients and gave the peptide in the morning. This was followed by a modified maintenance of wakefulness test, attention tests and another night in the sleep laboratory. Again, transitions from wakefulness into REM sleep and REM duration decreased, in the following night the light sleep stage N2 lengthened, and in the divided attention test the patients made fewer errors. An improvement in the maintenance of wakefulness test is not among the reported results.

Healthy men: sympathetic activity instead of wakefulness (2022)

The only study in healthy people so far comes from Lübeck. Ten lean young men received orexin A or placebo intranasally in a double-blind fashion; what was measured was the nerve activity that constricts the blood vessels in the muscles. It rose considerably more after orexin A, by 5.8 versus 2.1 bursts per minute. Blood pressure, heart rate and heart rate variability stayed the same. The authors interpret this as an effect on higher-level centers in the hypothalamus. Wakefulness or mental performance were not measured.

What arrives in the brain via the nose (2018)

Researchers from Sanofi and the Martinos Center in Boston labeled orexin A radioactively and tracked it by PET in rodents and monkeys. After intranasal administration, only a little of it reached the brain, in most brain regions about as much as after intravenous administration. Only in the olfactory bulb was uptake possibly higher. This dampens the expectation that the nose opens a direct route into the whole brain.

Where the data stop

The claim under which orexin A is sold – a wake-promoting agent for sleep loss – has never been tested in humans. There is no study with sleep-deprived healthy people, and the 2007 monkey experiment has not been repeated in humans. And in narcolepsy patients, who actually lack the peptide, wakefulness did not measurably improve with intranasal orexin A.

All four human studies gave a single dose to 7 to 14 participants, and three of them come from the same Kiel research group. Nothing is known about repeated use, tolerance, duration of effect or long-term consequences. ClinicalTrials.gov contains no study with the peptide as investigational substance.

The route itself is also open: the PET data argue against much arriving in the brain via the nose. And since August 2026 there has been a tested active substance at the orexin system, oveporexton, that works as a tablet. Whether an intranasal peptide would have any advantage over it has not been studied.

Status, approval and legal

Orexin A is not an approved medicine in Germany or the EU. As a food supplement, it would require authorization under the Novel Food Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, which does not exist. It is sold as a laboratory chemical for research purposes, sometimes as a nasal spray.

What is approved at the orexin system is a different substance: on August 5, 2026, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted approval for oveporexton under the name Orzeyful, for adults with narcolepsy type 1. The agency also names restrictions: not tested in minors, not together with strong CYP3A inhibitors, and classification under US controlled substances law had been recommended and was still pending at the time of approval.

In sport, the peptide falls under group S0 of the World Anti-Doping Agency: pharmacologically active substances without approval for therapeutic use in humans are prohibited at all times, even if they are not named on the list.

Safety

There are no systematic tolerability data. The four human studies each gave a single dose; nothing is known about repeated doses, longer use or interactions.

One finding deserves attention: in healthy men, orexin A increased the sympathetic activity that constricts blood vessels. Blood pressure and pulse remained unchanged in the measurement, but the authors consider a role of the orexin system in neurogenic high blood pressure worth investigating. The consequences of repeated doses are unknown.

What activation of the orexin system over weeks triggers has so far been shown only by the approved tablet drug: the most common effects were insomnia, more frequent urination, urinary urgency and increased salivation.

More important than any list of side effects is how the symptom is handled: persistent daytime sleepiness, sleep attacks or sudden muscle weakness with emotions call for evaluation by a sleep medicine specialist. Narcolepsy type 1 is treatable, and self-experimentation with laboratory material delays the diagnosis. With research-grade material, content, purity and sterility are also unverified.

Frequently asked questions about orexin A

What is orexin A?

Orexin A, also called hypocretin-1, is an endogenous neuropeptide from the hypothalamus. It activates both orexin receptors and keeps the waking state stable. In narcolepsy with cataplexy, it is greatly reduced or undetectable in the cerebrospinal fluid.

Are there studies on orexin A in humans?

Yes, but only a few small ones: four placebo-controlled crossover studies with a single intranasal dose, three in narcolepsy with 7, 8 and 14 patients and one with 10 healthy men. No study with the peptide is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Does intranasal orexin A make you awake?

That has not been shown in humans. In narcolepsy patients, it changed REM sleep and reduced errors in an attention test, but wakefulness did not measurably increase. The improved performance under sleep deprivation comes from a study in rhesus monkeys.

What is the difference between orexin A and orexin B?

Orexin A binds to both orexin receptors and, in animal experiments, passes intact from the blood into the brain. Orexin B prefers receptor 2, breaks down quickly in the blood and has never been studied in humans. All human studies with an orexin peptide used orexin A.

Is there an approved drug that targets the orexin system?

Yes, but it is not a peptide: on August 5, 2026, the FDA approved the oral orexin receptor 2 agonist oveporexton as Orzeyful for adults with narcolepsy type 1. The most common side effects were insomnia, more frequent urination, urinary urgency and increased salivation.

Is orexin A legal in Germany?

It is neither approved as a medicine nor authorized as a novel food and is sold as a laboratory peptide for research purposes. For athletes it counts as a non-approved substance in group S0 and is prohibited at all times.
