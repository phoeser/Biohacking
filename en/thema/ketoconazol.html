Ketoconazole shampoo for hair loss

Topical antifungal, available without prescription in pharmacies · Ketoconazole 2%, Terzolin, Ketozolin

Ketoconazole is an active ingredient against skin fungi that is available without prescription in pharmacies as a 2 percent shampoo. It is not approved for hereditary hair loss, but has been studied since the 1990s as an add-on to minoxidil and finasteride. Small studies show thicker hair and less hair loss. A placebo-controlled trial is still missing to this day.

What ketoconazole shampoo is

Ketoconazole belongs to the imidazole antifungals, active ingredients against fungi. As a 2 percent shampoo or solution it is approved in Germany for seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp and for pityriasis versicolor, without prescription but only in pharmacies. As a tablet, it now plays a role only in Cushing’s syndrome.

In hair loss research, the shampoo has appeared since the late 1990s: as an inexpensive add-on to minoxidil and finasteride, with a different point of attack. What is rated here is the state of knowledge. It is enough for a plausible hypothesis and some encouraging small studies, not for robust proof of efficacy.

How it works

In fungi, ketoconazole inhibits the formation of ergosterol, a building block of the cell membrane. On the scalp, this affects the Malassezia yeasts that are involved in dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis. The hypothesis for hair loss: inflammation around the hair follicles triggered by these yeasts could aggravate hereditary hair loss, and ketoconazole also has an anti-inflammatory effect.

An antiandrogenic effect is also frequently mentioned. What is established is that ketoconazole inhibits enzymes of steroid synthesis in the adrenal gland within the body; that is why it is approved as a tablet for Cushing’s syndrome. For the shampoo, such an effect on the scalp has not been shown. According to the prescribing information, ketoconazole is not measurable in the blood after application to the scalp, whereas it is after application to the whole body.

What is well supported

  • The shampoo is well tolerated and well studied. The safety of the shampoo was recorded in 22 clinical studies with 2,890 participants. In 2013 the EMA explicitly reviewed use on the skin and kept it authorized, because hardly any active ingredient enters the body.
  • Less hair loss and thicker hair with dandruff. In 150 men with hair loss and dandruff, 1 percent ketoconazole shampoo over six months reduced shedding hairs by 17.3 percent, the proportion of growing hairs rose by 4.9 percent, and the hair shafts became 5.4 percent thicker (Piérard-Franchimont 2002).
  • Several small studies point in the same direction. A systematic review found five human studies with 318 participants reporting thicker hair shafts and clinical improvement, plus two animal studies with more hair growth (Fields 2020).

What the studies show

Piérard-Franchimont 1998: the comparison with minoxidil

The research group compared 2 percent ketoconazole shampoo with a shampoo without active ingredient, each with or without 2 percent minoxidil. Hair density, hair thickness and the proportion of growing hairs improved to a similar extent under ketoconazole and under minoxidil, and the amount of sebum on the scalp decreased under ketoconazole. The authors interpreted this as an indication of a role for Malassezia yeasts and themselves called for larger controlled studies.

Piérard-Franchimont 2002: three anti-dandruff shampoos

150 men with hair loss and dandruff were randomly assigned to one of three shampoos with 1 percent ketoconazole, piroctone olamine or zinc pyrithione, over six months. All three quickly eliminated itching and dandruff. Hair density at the vertex remained unchanged, but hair loss during washing decreased, under ketoconazole by 17.3 percent. The hair shafts became 5.4 percent thicker under ketoconazole and 7.7 percent thicker under piroctone olamine. There was no group without active ingredient.

Khandpur 2002: ketoconazole as an add-on to finasteride

In an open-label randomized study with 100 men, dermatologists compared finasteride, minoxidil, both together, and finasteride plus ketoconazole shampoo over one year. Finasteride with minoxidil performed best, followed by finasteride with ketoconazole, then finasteride alone. All finasteride groups were significantly ahead of minoxidil alone. The ketoconazole group, however, had only 10 men, and assessment was based on ratings and photos, not blinded.

Fields 2020: the review

A systematic review included seven papers, five in humans with a total of 318 participants and two in mice. The human studies report thicker hair shafts, one study a higher hair index made up of growth proportion and hair thickness, plus improvements in photo assessment and self-assessment. The authors see ketoconazole as a promising add-on or alternative and consider randomized controlled trials necessary.

Where the data stop

  • No placebo-controlled trial. For hereditary hair loss, ketoconazole shampoo has never been tested in a blinded study with a shampoo without active ingredient as the control.
  • Hair density. In the largest study, the hair became thicker and less fell out, but the number of hairs did not increase. Whether ketoconazole makes new hair grow or mainly improves dandruff and inflammation is open.
  • People without dandruff. Many participants also had dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis. Whether the shampoo also helps against hereditary hair loss on a healthy scalp has hardly been studied.
  • Antiandrogenic effect. It is often mentioned, but has not been shown for application to the scalp; ketoconazole is not measurable in the blood afterwards.

Status, approval and legal

In Germany, ketoconazole is prescription-only, except for external use. Shampoo and solution at 2 percent are therefore available without prescription, but only in pharmacies. They are approved for seborrheic dermatitis and pityriasis versicolor, not for hair loss.

The EU suspended ketoconazole tablets for fungal infections in 2013 because of the risk of liver damage. In doing so, the EMA explicitly stated that creams, ointments and shampoos can continue to be used, because only very little active ingredient enters the body. Since 2014, ketoconazole tablets have been approved only for endogenous Cushing’s syndrome. Ketoconazole is not banned in sport.

Safety

The shampoo is well studied and considered well tolerated. The prescribing information lists skin irritation, burning, itching, dryness, acne, contact dermatitis and hypersensitivity reactions. Contact with the eyes should be avoided.

Notable for the topic of hair loss: the prescribing information itself lists hair loss, abnormal hair texture and hair discoloration among the possible side effects. Seborrheic dermatitis is often accompanied by hair loss; in rare cases this has also been observed with the shampoo. After long treatment with corticosteroid preparations, the prescribing information recommends a gradual transition to avoid a relapse.

Frequently asked questions about ketoconazole shampoo for hair loss

Does ketoconazole shampoo help against hair loss?

Possibly as an add-on; it is not proven. Small studies show thicker hair and less hair loss, in the largest, with 150 men, 17.3 percent fewer shedding hairs. Hair density did not increase there, and there is no placebo-controlled trial.

Does ketoconazole act as an antiandrogen?

As a tablet, ketoconazole inhibits enzymes of steroid synthesis in the adrenal gland and is therefore used for Cushing’s syndrome. For the shampoo, an antiandrogenic effect on the scalp has not been shown; after application to the scalp, no ketoconazole is measurable in the blood.

Is ketoconazole shampoo available without prescription?

Yes, for external use ketoconazole is exempt from the prescription requirement. The 2 percent shampoo is, however, pharmacy-only and approved for seborrheic dermatitis and pityriasis versicolor, not for hair loss.

Can ketoconazole shampoo itself cause hair loss?

The prescribing information lists hair loss, abnormal hair texture and hair discoloration among the possible side effects. Seborrheic dermatitis itself is often accompanied by hair loss; in rare cases this has also been observed with the shampoo.

Why were ketoconazole tablets taken off the market?

In 2013 the EU suspended ketoconazole tablets for fungal infections because of the risk of liver damage. Shampoos and creams were explicitly not affected, because only very little active ingredient enters the body with them.
