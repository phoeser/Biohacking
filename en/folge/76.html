Podcast episode 76 (in German)

Red light therapy: photobiomodulation fact-checked

September 14, 2026

Red panels now glow in every other studio, and the episode sorts out what is really supported. Photobiomodulation is not a heat lamp but light as a signal: narrow wavelengths that arrive in the mitochondria, with moderate but reproducible effects.

What it is about

The episode first separates two things that are constantly mixed up on the market: heat applications that act via temperature, and photobiomodulation, which works with narrow bands around 630 to 660 nanometres in the red and 800 to 850 nanometres in the near infrared, without significant warming. Then it goes through the areas of application for which controlled studies exist — skin, hair, muscle recovery, pain and joints — and explains why the dose decides everything: the response curve is biphasic, too much cancels the effect again. At the end come four criteria for choosing a device, the history of the method since 1967 and a sober classification as a fine-tuning tool with little risk.

Key points

  • Photobiomodulation works with narrow bands: red light around 630 to 660 nanometres, near infrared around 800 to 850 nanometres, at power levels that barely warm the tissue.
  • The most discussed target sits in the mitochondria: cytochrome c oxidase, complex 4 of the respiratory chain, absorbs exactly these wavelengths and is thought to release inhibitory nitric oxide.
  • The calculation is in joules per square centimetre, in research mostly in the single-digit to low double-digit range per session — according to the episode, 90 percent of home use fails on this quantity.
  • Typical in the studies: 5 to 15 minutes per body zone from a distance of 10 to 30 centimetres, 3 to 5 times a week.
  • Visible skin results need 8 to 12 weeks of consistent use; the before-and-after photo after 12 weeks is the most honest referee.
  • The method goes back to a chance finding in 1967: in irradiated mice the fur grew back faster, while the hoped-for cancer effect did not materialize.
  • In terms of price, the range extends from under 100 euros for masks to 20 to 50 euros per session in a studio’s full-body bed.

Where the data stop

The mechanism is plausible but not settled: besides cytochrome c oxidase, other explanations compete, up to structured cell water, and which dose is optimal for which tissue remains a matter of open research. For athletes there is a second uncertainty — whether a permanently applied recovery stimulus dampens the training stimulus is a timing question without a robust answer.

The second break lies between study and device: the lion’s share of the good data comes from small, targeted applications directly on the skin, not from the full-body glow of the advertising, and independent measurements show that some cheap panels deliver only a fraction of the advertised power into the tissue. And where the promises are greatest — cancer, Alzheimer’s, long Covid — the evidence stops; photobiomodulation of the brain is ongoing research, not a finished procedure.
