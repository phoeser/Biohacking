The alkaline bath – an old ritual, a look inside the cell and what warm water can really do

September 26, 2026

The most alkaline place in the body is not the bathtub but the interior of the mitochondria, with a pH of almost 8. The episode takes the alkaline bath ritual seriously, looks into the cell, where acid and base drive energy metabolism, and shows what warm bathing demonstrably achieves.

What it is about

In an alkaline bath, the bath water is brought to a pH of about 8.5 with alkaline salt; neutral would be 7. Users report velvety skin, genuine relaxation and fixed rituals during alkaline fasting, often two to three times a week. The episode explains what acid and base mean in and around the cell: why the interior of the mitochondria is alkaline, why lactate tends to act as a brake rather than causing acidosis, and what research says about mild acidosis in old age. Then it turns to the well-studied effects of a warm bath on sleep, heart and mood. It ends with a ritual for the evening and the reminder that bases get into the body via the plate.

Key points

  • Users report soft skin, relaxed muscles, a lighter feeling and better sleep. In a small study, baking soda baths eased itching and irritation in psoriasis, and participants kept bathing of their own accord after the study ended.
  • Inside the mitochondria the pH is almost 8: the respiratory chain pumps protons outward, and their flow back through ATP synthase supplies the cell’s energy. Peter Mitchell described this in 1961 against strong opposition and received the Nobel Prize in 1978.
  • A much-cited review sees lactate not as the cause of acidification during exercise but rather as a brake; well-trained mitochondria are the best acid buffer.
  • With age, acid in the blood rises slightly and bicarbonate falls. Potassium citrate increased bone density in two Swiss double-blind studies, in 161 postmenopausal women and in 201 people over 65; a Scottish study of 276 women found no difference.
  • Alkaline minerals taken by mouth reduced the pain score of 82 people with chronic back pain by 49 percent in 4 weeks, and magnesium in the cells rose by 11 percent – a strong signal, but without a comparison group.
  • In a Japanese cohort of more than 30,000 people, those who took a bath almost daily had 28 percent fewer cardiovascular diseases than those who bathed at most twice a week. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found that a warm bath or shower 1 to 2 hours before bedtime improves sleep.
  • In depression, 2 baths per week at 40 degrees improved symptoms after 4 baths considerably more than a sham treatment; compared with exercise, almost everyone stuck with bathing. In overweight men, fasting blood sugar and insulin fell after ten warm baths.

Where the data stop

That acids are drawn out of the body through the skin is not supported by evidence; there is no controlled study on the alkaline bath itself. The acid-base balance is regulated by the lungs and kidneys, and bases enter the body mainly via vegetables, fruit and minerals. The hypothesis of mild acidification in old age is research in progress: for potassium citrate and bone density there are two positive double-blind studies from Switzerland and one without a difference. The BK score stands at human evidence 6, mechanism 7, safety 7, hype gap 6 and application experience 8.

The proven effects belong to the warm water: sleep, circulation and mood. The studies on this mostly ran at around 40 degrees, i.e. warmer than the classic body-temperature alkaline bath. With dry or sensitive skin, shorter baths and moisturizing afterwards are worthwhile, because healthy skin is slightly acidic. Anyone with cardiovascular problems or who is pregnant should discuss long hot baths with a doctor; alkaline preparations in kidney disease belong in a physician’s hands.
