Podcast episode 97 (in German)

Sermorelin – an approved drug that no longer is one

October 5, 2026

Sales pages carry a sentence that is true: Sermorelin was approved by the US drug regulator. This episode shows what exactly for — and why, of all things, a correctly quoted sentence does not answer the question that matters when buying.

What it is about

Sermorelin is the front section of growth hormone–releasing hormone, the first 29 amino acids of GHRH, so not the hormone itself but the command for it. Its mechanism is considered the most sensible in this substance class: instead of overriding the body’s own system, Sermorelin addresses the pituitary gland, which then releases in its own rhythm. The episode goes through the real approval history, explains why the agency formally determined years after the market withdrawal that neither safety nor efficacy was the reason, and then follows the studies in older adults, from California and Seattle, and what users report today, above all about sleep.

Key points

  • Sermorelin consists of the first 29 amino acids of GHRH and acts as a signal to the pituitary gland, not as a replacement for the hormone itself.
  • Under the name Geref there were 2 approvals: in 1990 as a diagnostic agent for gland function and in 1997 for treating children with growth hormone deficiency.
  • In 2009 the product disappeared from the market; the agency’s formal determination that neither safety nor efficacy was the reason followed 4 years later and serves an administrative purpose.
  • In the large study in 110 children, growth velocity rose after six months from just over 4 to 8 centimeters a year; recombinant growth hormone nevertheless did better.
  • In California, skin thickness increased measurably in 19 women and men aged 55 to 71; the men gained lean mass, insulin sensitivity and well-being.
  • In the largest study, six months placebo-controlled in healthy older adults in Seattle, IGF-1 rose by about a third, lean mass increased, body fat decreased, and those treated did better in several cognitive tests; strength and endurance did not improve.
  • Users mainly report better sleep; in Seattle, by contrast, sleep became slightly worse on the questionnaire, though in people who already slept well at baseline. A study in poor sleepers is lacking.
  • The BK-Score stands at human evidence 4 since the Seattle study was included, mechanism 7, safety data 4, track record of use 4 and hype gap 2.

Where the data stop

For healthy adults, Sermorelin has not been disproven but is untested. For healthy adults there is a placebo-controlled six-month study in older people (Seattle) and smaller studies; long-term data are lacking. The approval studies concerned children with proven deficiency, and since 2009 no manufacturer has collected side-effect reports. What circulates today comes from compounding pharmacies and the gray market — a product without approval also no longer has a manufacturer vouching for its quality. And the safety profile the advertising relies on applies to a specific group under medical supervision, not to self-directed use over months.

Equally open is the objection that applies to anything that stimulates growth: growth hormone and IGF-1 promote cell growth, which is not a good property where tumors already exist. There are no long-term data on Sermorelin in healthy people on this point, neither reassuring nor worrying. And the advertised natural brake — that the gland only releases what it has — is at the same time the upper limit of the possible benefit.
