Podcast episode 56 (in German)

Oxytocin: the bonding hormone fact-checked

August 26, 2026

Oxytocin is known as a drug for inducing contractions in childbirth and as the “cuddle hormone”. Researchers in Berkeley showed that it also lets old muscles regenerate again, at least in mice. The episode links bonding and tissue repair and shows how to raise the hormone without any prescription.

What it is about

Oxytocin is a peptide hormone made of 9 amino acids, produced in the hypothalamus and used in the delivery room for almost 100 years, where it triggers contractions and milk flow. In the 2000s, its role in bonding and trust came into focus. The episode tells the lesser-known story: levels fall with age, and oxytocin is necessary for muscle regeneration. On top of that come findings on bone, heart, inflammation and the energy sensor AMPK, as well as an evolutionary explanation of why care and repair belong together. What remains at the end is a prescription-only hormone with a solid basis in animal data, and a free route via touch, community and warmth.

Key points

  • In the study by Kosfeld and colleagues in Nature in 2005, participants given a spray of oxytocin into the nose entrusted measurably more money to others in an investment game.
  • Elabd and colleagues from the lab of Irina and Michael Conboy showed in Nature Communications in 2014 that oxytocin awakens muscle stem cells via the MAPK signaling pathway; with it, old mice regenerated almost like young ones.
  • In young animals with full levels, additional oxytocin did nothing: it tops up an age-related deficit rather than acting beyond the normal level.
  • Oxytocin receptors sit on bone cells; in the heart the hormone promotes vascular relaxation via nitric oxide, and it dampens inflammatory messengers such as IL-6 and TNF.
  • Oxytocin rises measurably with hugs lasting from about 20 seconds, with massage, when singing in a choir, when stroking a dog, in warm baths and during deep conversations.
  • In the blood, oxytocin lasts only a few minutes and fluctuates strongly depending on the situation; even a hug before the blood draw changes the value, which is why it is of little use as a lab value.
  • Researchers at MIT found that the gut bacterium Lactobacillus reuteri stimulates oxytocin release via the vagus nerve; in mice, wounds healed faster as a result.

Where the data stop

The bodily effects on muscle, bone and heart rest on a solid animal and mechanistic basis; large human studies are lacking, as of August 2026. The psychological nasal spray studies partly have replication problems; the reputation as a “trust spray” was bigger than the data. In psychiatry and pain research the results are mixed or still young, and the same goes for the reuteri data in humans.

In Germany, oxytocin is prescription-only, nasal spray forms are supplied by compounding pharmacies, and medical use belongs in the hands of a physician. Occasionally reported are nasal irritation and head pressure, rarely a drop in blood pressure or water retention; in pregnancy it is off-limits outside obstetrics. Gray-market sprays raise questions of purity. The route via closeness and community knows none of these limits.
