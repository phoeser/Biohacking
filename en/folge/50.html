Podcast episode 50 (in German)

Spermidine: the cell-cleaning molecule fact-checked

August 21, 2026

Spermidine is found in every cell, its level falls with age, and in healthy people of very advanced age it often remains high. The substance triggers cellular cleaning the way fasting does, and it is found in wheat germ, aged cheese, mushrooms and natto.

What it is about

Spermidine is a polyamine, an ancient molecule that stabilizes DNA and helps with cell division and growth; Antoni van Leeuwenhoek described it as early as 1678. It is of interest to aging research because it triggers autophagy, the cell’s recycling program, for whose discovery the Nobel Prize was awarded in 2016. To do this, spermidine inhibits, among other things, the enzyme EP300, which slows autophagy, and is therefore considered a fasting mimetic. The episode goes through the Graz research led by Frank Madeo, a long-term study from South Tyrol and a disappointing memory study, and shows how to get spermidine onto your plate. At the end stands an honest longevity candidate: a clear mechanism, strong animal data, promising observations and a no on memory.

Key points

  • In a study in Nature Medicine in 2016, mice with spermidine in their drinking water lived around 10 percent longer, even when started late, and their aging hearts remained more elastic.
  • When autophagy was switched off genetically, the effect disappeared; from 2009 onwards, Madeo’s team in Graz had already shown longer life in yeast, flies and worms.
  • In the Bruneck study of more than 800 South Tyroleans followed for 20 years, people with a lot of spermidine in their diet had lower all-cause mortality, a calculated difference of about 5 years of life.
  • The SmartAge trial, published in 2022 in JAMA Network Open, found no significant difference in memory after 12 months of spermidine versus placebo in older people with memory concerns.
  • A typical Western diet supplies roughly 10 to 15 milligrams of spermidine a day; the top group in Bruneck was more likely at 20 or more.
  • A 2023 paper in Nature Aging showed that spermidine slows the aging of egg cells in mice; in Oxford, it improved the function of old T cells in the laboratory.
  • A small Italian study found that 3 months of spermidine prolonged the growth phase of hair follicles; plausible as a side effect, rather thin as a main reason.

Where the data stop

The human data are mainly observational: people who eat a lot of spermidine usually eat better overall, so Bruneck shows an association, not proof. Where measurements were rigorous, in SmartAge, there was no benefit for memory; however, the dose there, at just under 1 milligram daily, was below what the dietary data suggest, and studies with higher doses are underway, as of August 2026. A benefit for the brain is therefore not proven.

Safety was very good in all human studies; spermidine is, after all, a component of food. In cancer, the topic belongs in medical consultation because fast-growing cells love polyamines, and people with celiac disease should look for gluten-free versions of wheat germ extracts. The foundation remains the same: breaks from eating, exercise and sleep.
