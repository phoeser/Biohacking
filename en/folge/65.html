Glutathione: the master antioxidant fact-checked

September 4, 2026

Among experts, glutathione is called the master antioxidant, and the title is deserved: without this tripeptide no cell survives for long. The episode explains why, of all things, swallowing the finished molecule is the most cumbersome route there.

What it is about

Glutathione is a tiny tripeptide made of glutamate, cysteine and glycine that every cell builds itself. It defuses free radicals, regenerates vitamins C and E, is the key substance of the second phase of liver detoxification and keeps the mitochondria running. Over the years the level drops considerably, and research now discusses this as an active driver of aging. From there, the episode goes through three routes: swallowing the finished molecule, supplying the building blocks and stimulating production itself, or cutting down the consumption side. Along the way it clears up the glamour track of infusions and skin-lightening promises and ends with an unusual recommendation: your own factory beats the expensive end product.

Key points

  • The substance was described as early as 1888 by Rey-Pailhade as philothion, the sulfur friend; for around 130 years it has been at the center of cell research.
  • Glutathione is a tripeptide of 3 amino acids and the central currency of the cell’s protective system — among other things in phase 2 of liver detoxification, where pollutants are coupled to it and excreted.
  • Richie’s 2015 study, randomized and placebo-controlled over 6 months with 250 to 1,000 milligrams daily, showed rising glutathione levels in blood cells of up to around 30 percent.
  • Sekhar and colleagues at Baylor College gave older adults glycine plus N-acetylcysteine for 16 weeks: glutathione normalized almost to youthful levels, oxidative stress fell, and gait speed and strength increased.
  • Selenium is the spark plug of the system, because the glutathione enzymes are selenium-dependent — 2 Brazil nuts cover the daily requirement, and whey protein additionally supplies plenty of cysteine.
  • The routes tested in studies: the GlyNAC pattern with 600 to 1,200 milligrams of NAC plus 2 to 5 grams of glycine daily, or directly 250 to 1,000 milligrams of glutathione for at least 8 to 12 weeks.

Where the data stop

The dispute over oral absorption has calmed down but is not over: a tripeptide is readily broken down in the gut, and the data on liposomal and acetylated forms are thin so far. The GlyNAC findings form a neat mechanistic whole, but come from small studies. Glutathione infusions flood the level for hours, but as a lifestyle subscription they have no long-term evidence, and the skin-lightening trend exploits a side effect with unclear safety in continuous use.

Then there is the hormesis question: the training stimulus works partly via free radicals, and studies around Michael Ristow showed that high-dose antioxidants around training can dampen adaptation. Measuring the status is optional, not mandatory, because the value is sensitive to handling and fluctuates. With severe liver and kidney disease, in pregnancy and during chemotherapy, the topic belongs in the hands of a physician.
