Podcast episode 127 (in German)

Hearing and dementia – the statement that holds, and the study that missed its goal

November 4, 2026

Hearing loss is the largest modifiable risk factor for dementia, and this statement still holds in the latest version. The large study on hearing aids nevertheless missed its primary goal. This episode shows why the two together are good news.

What it is about

The statement comes from the dementia commission of the journal Lancet, which calculates what share of dementia cases is attributable to modifiable factors. Hearing loss shares the top spot there, but as a population share, not as a personal risk. The episode explains this difference, the pathways by which poor hearing could harm the brain, and what the first large randomized trial with hearing aids showed: no difference overall, but a clear one in a prespecified risk group. Added to this are new observational data and what people with hearing aids say themselves. At the end stands a practical point: for hearing itself, the benefit has long been established, even if the question about the brain remains open.

Key points

  • In 2020 the Lancet Commission counted 12 modifiable factors together accounting for 40 percent of dementia cases, with hearing loss ahead at 8 percent; in 2024 it is 14 factors and 45 percent.
  • In 2024, hearing loss at 7 percent shares the top spot with high LDL cholesterol; education and social isolation follow at 5 percent each, high blood pressure at 2.
  • The ACHIEVE study assigned 977 people aged 70 to 84 at 4 centers in the US to hearing aids with counseling or to a health education program; after 3 years, both groups lost almost the same amount of cognitive performance.
  • In 238 older participants with more risk factors, hearing care slowed decline by 48 percent; the analysis was prespecified, and its control group declined 2.7 times as much as that of the 739 healthy volunteers.
  • In an Australian analysis of just over 2,700 older people over 7 years, dementia occurred in 5 percent with a hearing aid and in 7.5 percent without; the authors cannot rule out confounding factors.
  • Among 61,000 people from 33 countries, a small association was found, and only in those who said they really hear better with the device.
  • In a survey of 653 hearing aid users, 80 percent of 2,122 counted statements described a gain, above all better relationships and easier work; a Cochrane review of 16 studies confirms a large benefit for understanding and participation.

Where the data stop

The 7 percent indicate how many dementia cases would disappear if nobody had untreated poor hearing anymore; they are large because hearing loss is so common, and say little about the individual. Whether poor hearing harms the brain or vascular damage affects ear and brain at the same time cannot be decided by observation alone. The 48 percent apply to a subgroup: a pointer for the next study, not a result for everyone. That with a hearing aid one more person remained in the social network over 3 years is measured; whether that matters in everyday life is open.

The experience reports come from a hearing aid community, a manufacturer’s customer database and just over 5,000 online comments; those who put the device in a drawer are probably missing there. Still, they describe what no cognitive scale measures: that life becomes bigger again with the device. The most frequent advice was not to wait, and in an American cohort it took an average of 8.9 years until people got fitted. Anyone who notices their hearing declining should first have it measured at an ENT practice.
