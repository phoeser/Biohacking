Podcast episode 87 (in German)

Peptides in cancer therapy – homing missiles, vaccines and a helper for the immune system

September 25, 2026

Peptides are making history in cancer medicine right now: as hormone blockers, as homing missiles with a radioactive payload, as vaccines in trials — and with thymosin alpha-1 as a possible helper meant to rebuild the immune system after chemotherapy and radiation.

What it is about

The episode begins with a race at the end of the 1960s: two research groups extracted one milligram of a hormone each from millions of pieces of sheep and pig brain and received the Nobel Prize for it in 1977. From there it goes through the roles that peptides play in oncology: GnRH analogs such as leuprorelin deprive hormone-dependent tumors of their growth signal, and somatostatin analogs such as lanreotide slow down neuroendocrine tumors — told through the example of a British patient who has received an injection every 28 days since 2010 and writes about it publicly. Radioligand therapy attaches a tiny amount of lutetium to a peptide and delivers the radiation directly to the tumor cell; Lutathera and Pluvicto, which was developed in Heidelberg, are approved, and the Zentralklinik Bad Berka in Thuringia was among the first hospitals to use it. Added to this are peptide vaccines and thymosin alpha-1, which is not meant to cure cancer but to support the body’s defenses during and after chemotherapy and radiation.

Key points

  • GnRH analogs such as leuprorelin and somatostatin analogs such as octreotide and lanreotide are peptides and have been guideline-based standard therapy for certain tumors for decades.
  • A British patient with a metastatic neuroendocrine tumor has received a lanreotide injection every 28 days since December 2010; he writes that he has not had a single flushing attack since the first injection and feels well most of the time.
  • In the pivotal NETTER-1 trial with 229 patients, Lutathera reduced the risk of progression or death by 79 percent; in NETTER-2, as first-line treatment, the disease remained stable for almost 23 instead of 8.5 months.
  • The active substance in Pluvicto was developed at the German Cancer Research Center in Heidelberg; Bad Berka was among the first clinics to treat patients with lutetium-PSMA from 2013 onward. In the VISION trial the men lived a median of 15.3 instead of 11.3 months, and at the end of July 2026 the FDA also approved Pluvicto for the earlier, hormone-sensitive stage.
  • In the first Australian study with 30 men, pain improved at every time point, with a mildly dry mouth being common; a case series describes three men over 90 whose PSA level fell by up to 98 percent under Pluvicto, with mild side effects.
  • A peptide vaccine added to immunotherapy extended the time without progression in melanoma in 407 patients to 19.4 instead of 11 months and narrowly missed its pre-specified statistical target; in tumors without PD-L1, it was 16.6 versus 3.0 months. The study was published in the Annals of Oncology in 2026.
  • Under thymosin alpha-1, a severe drop in immune cells during chemoradiotherapy for lung cancer occurred in 19 instead of 62 percent; in a European melanoma study with 488 patients there was more tumor response without additional side effects. In the BK score it stands at human evidence 7, safety 7 and application 6.

Where the data stop

The case reports are individual cases and not studies; they show what is possible, not what can be expected. The data on thymosin alpha-1 come mostly from China and often from retrospective analyses; in the lung cancer study the comparison group was assembled after the fact, and the survival benefit in the melanoma study narrowly missed statistical significance. In Germany, thymosin alpha-1 is not approved; in more than thirty other countries it is. Peptide vaccines are still mostly investigational medicine.

Anyone interested in one of these therapies should discuss it with their treatment team — precisely because they interact with chemotherapy, radiation and immunotherapy. This page does not replace such a conversation and does not make a diagnosis. The Cancer Information Service of the German Cancer Research Center answers questions independently and free of charge by phone or email.
