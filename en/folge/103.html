Podcast episode 103 (in German)

Cardio after menopause – why Zone 2 was never tested

October 11, 2026

In the year around the final menstrual period, the aorta stiffens much faster than in the years before — and the scale shows none of it. This episode puts into context what changes in the cardiovascular system, what endurance training demonstrably achieves in this phase of life, and why the easy pace has long been tested, even if it was not yet called Zone 2 back then.

What it is about

Around the final menstrual period, several things shift at once: total cholesterol, LDL and apolipoprotein B make their own jump, the aorta stiffens faster, and fat accumulates twice as fast. Blood glucose, blood pressure and inflammatory markers, by contrast, simply follow age, and weight keeps rising steadily. Then it turns to what holds up: active women with more supple arteries, fewer cardiovascular events with plenty of movement, a dose–response study at an easy pace with better sleep. Women describe how they adapt their training to menopause. Finally, the question of why Zone 2 in particular has never been specifically tested in this group, which explicitly does not mean it does not work.

Key points

  • In the US long-term SWAN study with more than 3,000 women, the aorta stiffened by 7.5 percent in the year around the final period, and by just under 1 percent in the years before; from the start of the transition, fat accumulates twice as fast and lean mass declines.
  • In Colorado, the aorta was clearly stiffer after menopause in sedentary women, but not in very active women — a small snapshot.
  • In the Women's Health Initiative with just over 73,000 women, the most active fifth had about a quarter fewer cardiovascular events; walking was associated with a similarly large benefit as vigorous exercise.
  • In Dallas, 464 inactive women after menopause trained for six months at half their peak capacity: 72 minutes per week brought 4.2 percent more oxygen uptake, 192 minutes 8.2 percent; even the smallest amount reduced the odds of clearly poor sleep to about a third.
  • In 5,721 asymptomatic women, each additional fitness level on the treadmill was associated with a 17 percent lower risk of death (Gulati 2003, observational).
  • A query of the trial registry for Zone 2 and menopause returns 0 hits; but the easy pace in Dallas is close to what is called Zone 2 today. The BK-Score for Zone 2 stands at human evidence 6, safety data and track record of use 9 each.
  • Strength and cardio interfere with each other only for explosive strength, an analysis of 43 studies shows — with 3 hours between them, this effect also disappears.
  • In a registry of more than 1 million heart attacks, 42 percent of women had no chest pain, versus 30.7 percent of men.

Where the data stop

Zone 2 is not disproven for women after menopause but has never been specifically tested: only a handful of studies on Zone 2 training are registered, none in this group, and a shared definition was only written down in 2025 by fourteen sports scientists and coaches, just below the first threshold. The pace itself, however, is old and has delivered. Several of the nicest findings are observations or snapshots, though, and the sleep finding from Dallas was only analyzed afterwards.

On hot flashes, a careful study is more reserved: twelve weeks of endurance training did not make them less frequent on average in 248 women, while sleep and mood improved slightly. Another open piece of the puzzle comes from a small study: in 36 estrogen-deficient women, the inner lining of the arteries improved with endurance training only when they also received estrogen. Anyone who suddenly feels unusual breathlessness, nausea or leaden exhaustion during exercise takes it seriously, and anyone with heart problems has the start checked by a physician.
