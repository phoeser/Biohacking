Strength training after menopause – the bridge nobody has proven

October 9, 2026

Light weights, many repetitions, just be careful with bone loss: this old rule of thumb does not hold up against the studies. The episode shows what heavy strength training during and after menopause does for muscles, bones, blood pressure and even hot flashes, what women say about it, and where the bridge to fewer fractures is still only half built.

What it is about

First the body: muscle mass is lost across the transition, measured rather than estimated, and it does not come back on its own. Then the bones, and there the study everyone cites — heavy lifting with squats, deadlifts and overhead presses in women with osteopenia and osteoporosis, with a clear gain at the lumbar spine, and replicated once. Then new data from Canada and a 2026 meta-analysis, followed by the surprising part: in Linköping, hot flashes became much less frequent with strength training, and the women explain why they stayed. Finally, the open question of fractures and the limits the German guideline sets.

Key points

  • Across the transition, women lose 1.1 percent of muscle mass in the arms and legs in just over 15 months, measured in 234 women — and this step does not come back on its own.
  • In the most-cited study, 101 women with osteopenia or osteoporosis gained 2.9 percent at the lumbar spine; the comparison group with light dumbbells lost 1.2 percent.
  • A replication by the same research group in 115 women came to 1.9 versus 0.1 percent; in 8 months of heavy-load training there was 1 adverse event.
  • New from Canada: 40 women in or shortly after the transition gained strength, almost 1 kilo of lean mass and lumbar spine density over 9 months of heavy training; a 2026 meta-analysis finds small, clear effects at the femoral neck and lumbar spine after menopause.
  • In Linköping, in 58 women with at least 4 strong hot flashes a day, moderate and severe flashes fell by almost 44 percent after 15 weeks of machine training, versus 2 percent in the control group; sleep problems also decreased.
  • Interviews afterwards: the women came because of the heat and stayed because it did them good; after one year, about half were still training with weights.
  • Across 11 studies, exercise brought about a quarter fewer major osteoporotic fractures, rate ratio 0.75; in 20 studies, fracture risk fell to 44 percent under supervised programs, but only to 83 under unsupervised ones.
  • The BK-Score for strength training stands at human evidence 9, safety data 9 and track record of use 10 — the best-supported single measure in the database.

Where the data stop

What remains open is the bridge between two well-supported banks. In the fracture studies, training was mostly mixed, with balance, tai chi, brisk walking and elastic bands; the studies with heavy lifting measured bone density but did not count fractures. That heavy loads also prevent fractures has not yet been shown directly, and the heavy-load data come largely from one research group.

The German S3 guideline does not wait for this: after menopause, women should train to prevent fractures, ideally supervised and at least twice a week. But it sets clear limits. With osteoporosis, no training to muscle failure and no depth jumps; anyone with vertebral fractures should not jump and should not train with intensity-focused high loads on the spine. Bending the back under load and straining while holding the breath should be avoided. It nowhere lists deadlifts as contraindicated; it depends on load, technique and history.
