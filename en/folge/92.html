Podcast episode 92 (in German)

PE-22-28 – the antidepressant that never reached a human

September 30, 2026

When a protein is trimmed in the body, a tiny piece falls off – and this very scrap led researchers in southern France to a new mood switch. PE-22-28 is its shortened version, from a line of research almost 20 years old with a first trace in human blood.

What it is about

It all begins in 2006 with mice lacking a particular potassium channel in the brain — TREK-1. In the usual tests, they behave as if they had been given an antidepressant. In 2010, a French group described spadin, an endogenous fragment that blocks this channel; in 2017 came PE-22-28, a shortened, stronger and longer-acting version. The episode explains why a new target in depression is needed, what the mouse data show up to the stroke model, and which trace exists in humans: the endogenous precursor fragment can be measured in the blood and moves with mood. Plus reports from users and the question of why no one has yet tested the peptide itself in humans.

Key points

  • The idea goes back to a 2006 observation: mice lacking the potassium channel TREK-1 do better in five depression models, without any medication; above all, serotonin transmission ran more strongly.
  • In 2010, Jean Mazella’s group described spadin, a fragment left over when the receptor sortilin is trimmed, which blocks exactly this channel. The shortened version of 7 amino acids presented in 2017 blocks it much more strongly, at 0.12 nanomolar versus 40 to 60 for spadin, and lasts 23 instead of 7 hours in the body.
  • In animal experiments the effect appeared after 4 days, whereas common antidepressants take 3 to 4 weeks in humans — two measures that are not directly comparable. In a mouse model of depression after stroke, the shortened version prevented depression-like behavior and improved movement and memory.
  • There is a trace in humans via the endogenous precursor: in severe depression, it was lower in the blood than in healthy people and rose again under treatment; in 45 patients receiving electroconvulsive therapy, it rose only in those who improved. In 204 people after a stroke, the level was linked to depression scores. What was measured was the endogenous fragment, not PE-22-28.
  • For PE-22-28 itself, there is no study in humans in the literature or trial registries. The target is still being pursued, though: in 2025, the company Ono and Vanderbilt University presented small molecules that block TREK channels, so far tested in rats.
  • In around 40 collected user reports, the most frequently mentioned effects are more drive and a brighter outlook, often within the first days; the most common complaint is tiredness. Without blinding, it is impossible to separate effect from expectation.
  • In the BK score, PE-22-28 stands at human evidence 0, mechanism 4 and safety data 1. The zero means: not yet tested in humans – not: failed.

Where the data stop

Everything known about PE-22-28 itself comes from cell and animal experiments. There is no safety profile in humans, no known interactions and no tested use; the blood levels of the endogenous precursor are observations, not treatment. The missing step would be a small phase 1 study with 20 to 30 healthy volunteers on tolerability and blood levels. The peptide is sold as a research chemical, and the contents of the vials are unverified.

There is also a limit that appears on no side-effect list. Depression is very treatable, with psychotherapy, with medications tested in humans, usually with both. Weeks or months with an untested peptide are weeks or months without the treatment that helps. If the topic affects you, the next step is an appointment with your GP or a psychotherapist; crisis helplines are available around the clock.
