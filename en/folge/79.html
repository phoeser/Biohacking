Podcast episode 79 (in German)

CGM: the blood glucose sensor fact-checked

September 17, 2026

Small white buttons on the upper arm, at the gym, in the office, at the bakery — and very few of the wearers have diabetes. The episode sorts out what a glucose sensor really shows in healthy people and where the hype has outrun the data.

What it is about

A hair-thin sensor filament measures glucose not in the blood but in the tissue fluid between the cells, every few minutes, over 10 to 15 days. This makes visible what otherwise remains invisible: how high the curve rises after eating, how long it stays up, what a walk afterwards does and what the night looks like. The episode explains the four metrics to start with, shows why two people produce two different curves from the same banana, and describes the time-limited self-experiment with a protocol, after which the arm may be free again, because the lessons remain.

Key points

  • The sensor measures in the tissue fluid, lags behind the blood by a few minutes and has a measurement tolerance of roughly 10 percent — built for trends, not for the decimal place.
  • Four numbers are enough to start: fasting value, peaks after eating, variability and the share of the day within the target range.
  • A fasting value persistently above 100 milligrams per decilitre is a reason to talk to a doctor; in metabolically healthy people, peaks usually stay below 140 and subside within 2 to 3 hours.
  • Since 2024, over-the-counter sensors made specifically for non-diabetics have been available in the US; in Germany, the classic ones are available without prescription in pharmacies.
  • A sensor with a 2-week lifespan costs around 50 to 80 euros; subscription models with app coaching charge considerably more.
  • As a learning experiment, the scene describes 2 to 4 weeks with 1 to 2 sensors: first 1 week of observation only, then deliberately change only one variable at a time.
  • The most reliable curve smoother is a 10-minute walk after eating; after hard interval training, by contrast, the value rises without any food at all.

Where the data stop

For people with diabetes, continuous monitoring is a blessing with hard evidence. For healthy people, there is so far no study showing that wearing a sensor improves weight, health or even lifespan in the long term. There are plausible mechanisms and impressive individual cases, nothing more.

Added to this are three objections that should be taken seriously. Healthy people tend to pathologize harmless fluctuations — a peak after eating is physiology, not a defect. Anyone who rates every food by its curve can slide into an eating-disorder dynamic; anyone prone to that is better off staying away. And the sensors are built for precision in the diabetic range: in the normal range their errors are relatively larger, and two sensors on the same arm not infrequently show different values. Abnormal values, suspected diabetes and a history of eating disorders call for medical supervision.
