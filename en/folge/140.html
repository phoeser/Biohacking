Citrus bergamot – the “natural statin” from Calabria and what it can really do

November 17, 2026

The tin says natural statin, plus almost 40 percent less LDL cholesterol in one month. The figure really exists, it just comes from those who own the product. In cleaner studies, a smaller but real effect remains.

What it is about

Bergamot is known as the scent in Earl Grey; more than 90 percent of world production grows on a narrow coastal strip in Calabria. In 2009, chemists described 2 molecules in the juice that at one corner look like a statin, hence the slogan. The episode traces the famous figure back to its origin, sets the studies from outside Italy alongside it and reads the new meta-analysis from September 2026. It also asks who buys something like this: people who do not tolerate statins and are looking for a way that does not hurt. Satisfied users report LDL reductions that match the studies, others notice nothing. In the end there is an effect of around 10 percent whose benefit for the heart nobody has yet measured.

Key points

  • The original figure comes from a 2011 study by the University of Catanzaro, known from a review: LDL minus 38.6 and HDL plus 39 percent in 30 days; the first author is an inventor on the extract’s patent.
  • In Shanghai in 2017, LDL fell by just under 9 percent compared with placebo in 98 older people after 12 weeks; in 108 adults from East Asia it fell by 7 percent, while under placebo it rose by a little more than 3.
  • The September 2026 meta-analysis pools 9 studies with 615 people, 8 of them from Italy: the larger the study, the smaller the effect, and 6 of 9 are considered methodologically weak.
  • In 2021, a group in Chicago tested the extract and its 5 main components on liver cells: none directly inhibited the statin enzyme, not even the two molecules that gave it its name.
  • In 5,328 Reddit posts about statins, analyzed in April 2026, just under half are about alternatives and almost a third about side effects, led by muscle pain.
  • As a report: of 24 ratings over 9 years on an American health website, a little more than half give 4 or 5 stars and mention LDL reductions of 10 to 20 percent; almost 4 in 10 give one star.
  • An Austrian man who had drunk 4 liters of black tea a day for 25 years developed muscle cramps after switching to Earl Grey, according to a 2002 case report; they disappeared again with black tea.

Where the data stop

The direction is the same in several controlled studies, but according to the meta-analysis the certainty is very low. The big figures come almost entirely from Italy, often from close to the manufacturers; the East Asian study and an Australian one without a difference were also paid for by manufacturers. No study has counted heart attacks. For statins this is known from studies with 170,000 people; for bergamot this step is missing.

The satisfied users tend to be at the upper end of the studies; many changed their diet at the same time, and lab values also drift without an active ingredient. The oil from the peel can make the skin sensitive to sunlight, and the juice contains bergamottin, which can slow the breakdown of some statins. The patented extract largely removes these substances; for other products there is no information. Anyone already taking medication for cholesterol or high blood pressure discusses the capsule with a doctor beforehand and does not swap a prescribed statin for it.
