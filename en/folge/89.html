Lipoprotein(a) – the trial that failed on September 4

September 27, 2026

On September 4, the HORIZON trial missed its goal: pelacarsen lowered lipoprotein(a), but not the number of heart attacks. The value itself remains one of the most cleanly established risk factors in cardiology – and one blood test is enough for a lifetime.

What it is about

Lipoprotein(a) is an LDL particle with an attachment: the long, twisted apolipoprotein(a), which makes it stickier and apparently more pro-inflammatory. The level is genetically determined, fixed at birth and practically cannot be shifted by diet, exercise or statins. About one in five people has an elevated level without knowing it, because it is not measured in the standard cholesterol check. Pelacarsen was meant to change that, preventing the attachment from being built in the liver in the first place. In the Lipoprotein(a)-HORIZON trial with 8,323 patients, the level did fall markedly — but the number of cardiovascular events did not. The episode goes through three explanations and sorts out what this means for your own prevention.

Key points

  • Lipoprotein(a) was described in 1963 by the Norwegian geneticist Kåre Berg. The level is largely inherited, hardly changes over a lifetime, and one measurement in a lifetime is enough; it is not included in the standard cholesterol check. Since March 2026, the new US guideline recommends measuring it in all adults.
  • About one in five people has an elevated level. In Copenhagen, among around 40,000 people, the top 5 percent had a risk of heart attack about two and a half times higher than those with the lowest levels – and because the level is inherited, this can be read like an experiment of nature.
  • In Lipoprotein(a)-HORIZON, 8,323 patients with established cardiovascular disease and levels of 70 mg/dl or more received pelacarsen; the primary endpoint of cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke and urgent procedures was missed in the overall group. Ionis speaks of an acceptable safety profile; there are no figures yet for the pre-specified group with 90 mg/dl or more.
  • As early as 2018, Burgess and Ference calculated that an absolute reduction of a good 101 mg/dl would be needed to match the risk reduction of lowering LDL by just under 39 mg/dl. Even an 80 percent reduction amounts to only 56 mg/dl from 70, and 72 from 90.
  • There are signs that lowering can help: on evolocumab, people with levels above the median benefited more (23 instead of 7 percent fewer events), and on alirocumab, the reduction in the level on its own predicted a lower risk. In Germany, the event rate in 170 patients on lipoprotein apheresis fell from 0.58 to 0.11 per year, without a control group.
  • In a discussion group with affected people, most had only learned of their level after a heart attack or because of their family; many described above all relief at having an explanation and wished for a dedicated contact person.
  • Three more drugs are in large trials: olpasiran with results around 2028, lepodisiran around 2029 and muvalaplin, which is given as a tablet. In the BK score, lipoprotein(a) stands at human evidence 8; the negative direction applies to lowering as a treatment, not to the level as a warning sign.

Where the data stop

It is established that lipoprotein(a) contributes to the damage. It is not established that lowering it later reverses that damage — two different statements. There is currently no drug proven to prevent heart attacks by lowering lipoprotein(a). Until the full HORIZON data are out, the press release is all there is; they will be presented on November 7, 2026, at the American Heart Association’s Scientific Sessions in Chicago, together with extended analyses.

Why HORIZON failed is open. It is conceivable that the baseline level of 70 mg/dl or more was too low to achieve enough absolute reduction: an 80 percent reduction from 70 is only 56 mg/dl – far below the roughly 101 needed. It is also conceivable that the participants, already comprehensively treated, left little room, or that decades of high levels leave vascular changes that a few years of medication can no longer reverse. Getting measured remains worthwhile, especially with early heart attacks in the family – a risk factor you cannot change is a good reason to take LDL, blood pressure, smoking and exercise more seriously.
