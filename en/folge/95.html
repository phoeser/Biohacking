Podcast episode 95 (in German)

HCG – a question answered 31 years ago

October 3, 2026

An analysis from 1995 answered the question of whether HCG does anything for weight loss. The answer is no, it is 31 years old, and the business carries on regardless. This episode explains why that works.

What it is about

HCG is a real, prescription-only medicine: in fertility treatment it triggers ovulation, and in certain forms of testosterone deficiency it gets the body’s own production going. In the mid-1950s, a doctor in Rome turned it into a weight-loss cure — a daily injection plus a diet of 500 kilocalories, about a quarter of what the body needs. The episode first shows what HCG achieves as a medicine, including in men on testosterone therapy. It then tells the story of the weight-loss cure as a genuine lead that was followed up several times, and separates the two halves of the cure: the injection contributes nothing, the 500-calorie diet works and is risky. It also covers why drops sold without a prescription cannot reach their target pharmacologically at all.

Key points

  • HCG is an approved medicine: it triggers ovulation in fertility treatment, is used for undescended testes and restarts the body's own production in men whose pituitary sends too little signal.
  • During testosterone therapy, HCG stands in for the missing signal: in 29 healthy men, testosterone in the testis fell by 94 percent without HCG and stayed in the normal range with a low HCG dose; in a practice analysis from Houston, none of 26 men lost their sperm. A controlled fertility study is still lacking.
  • The weight-loss cure goes back to the physician Albert Simeons, who described it in the Lancet in 1954: an injection almost every day plus 500 kilocalories a day, roughly a quarter of what the body needs.
  • In 1973 a controlled US study did find more weight loss and less hunger under HCG. A double-blind replication with 51 women, a study with 202 participants and a German study with 82 women found no difference.
  • An analysis from Amsterdam in 1995 pooled 24 studies: of the 12 methodologically best, all controlled, only one saw an advantage. The weight loss comes from the diet.
  • The BK-Score gives human evidence 8 and a 9 each for mechanism, safety data and track record of use, because of the approved uses and decades of medical practice, not because of the diet studies.
  • Drops sold without a prescription fail on biochemistry: HCG is a large protein molecule and is digested when swallowed, and many products are also homeopathically diluted — what remains is the 500-calorie diet.

Where the data stop

This is not where the data stop — this is where the attribution is wrong. The concluding sentence of the 1995 analysis is unambiguous: no scientific evidence that HCG causes weight loss, redistributes fat, keeps hunger at bay or creates a sense of well-being. Such clarity is rare in this series.

Something else remains open: using HCG to maintain the body’s own production during testosterone therapy is pharmacologically plausible and is mostly prescribed off-label; controlled fertility data are lacking. That is the same substance for a different question and not an argument for the diet. Safety covers both halves: an intake of 500 kilocalories over weeks is malnutrition, with muscle loss, gallstones and electrolyte disturbances up to cardiac arrhythmias — the US drug regulator has explicitly warned that this can be fatal. And the hormone itself has side effects that put it in a physician’s hands.
