Resveratrol – rise and fall of a longevity star

September 24, 2026

A defensive substance from Japanese knotweed and grapes became one of the great hopes of longevity research. The episode tells the long journey of resveratrol – with highs, a few lows and twists that are often overlooked.

What it is about

The rise unfolded in three acts: the French paradox of the nineties, the discovery of sirtuins at MIT and the 2003 finding that resveratrol activates these enzymes. In 2006 came the Nature paper according to which resveratrol extends the life of mice fed a high-fat diet, and in 2008 GlaxoSmithKline bought Sirtris for 720 million dollars. After that, the picture became mixed: a dispute over the assay used in the sirtuin tests that later turned out to be a lead, mice on a normal diet that did not live longer but healthier, and a little-known study in postmenopausal women with positive results for cognition, bones and well-being. The episode also covers where the data stop: blood sugar, blood lipids and training.

Key points

  • In 2003, David Sinclair’s team reported that resveratrol boosts sirtuins and that yeast cells live 70 percent longer; in 2006, mice on a very fatty diet lived longer with resveratrol. In 2008, GlaxoSmithKline bought Sinclair’s company Sirtris for 720 million dollars.
  • Several labs showed that sirtuin activation in the test tube depended on a fluorescent dye in the assay. In 2013, a paper in Science explained why: the dye mimics a water-repellent site that real target proteins also carry – with such substrates, the enzyme can be activated directly.
  • Mice on a normal diet did not live longer, neither in Sinclair’s follow-up study nor in the independent US testing program. The old animals were healthier, though: a more elastic aorta, better coordination, fewer cataracts and more stable bones.
  • In a double-blind crossover study at the University of Newcastle in Australia with 125 postmenopausal women over two years, participants scored a third better on the overall measure of thinking and memory while on resveratrol. Bone density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck was higher, a bone-loss marker fell by just over 7 percent, and the women reported less chronic pain and greater well-being.
  • Eleven overweight men showed a more economical metabolism during sleep, less liver fat and slightly lower blood sugar and blood pressure after one month of resveratrol – similar to calorie restriction, according to the authors. In an Alzheimer’s study with 119 participants, resveratrol reached the cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Where marketing was loudest, the data do not hold up: a review of ten meta-analyses in type 2 diabetes found no consistent effect on blood sugar and blood lipids, and in older men, endurance improved considerably more with training on placebo than with resveratrol.
  • In the BK score, resveratrol stands at human evidence 4, safety data 7, application experience 7 and hype gap 1: the promise of a fountain of youth floats far above the data, while the smaller, real findings are hardly known.

Where the data stop

The human studies are small and short and rely on blood values, tests and questionnaires; there is no large study with a hard endpoint such as lifespan. Absorption remains the biggest stumbling block: the gut and liver convert resveratrol quickly, and hardly any free compound is found in the blood. The converted forms do, however, circulate as a kind of depot from which some cells release resveratrol again; whether that is enough remains open. The results of the Australian study in postmenopausal women are still waiting for a large follow-up study.

Anyone who notices something notices it quietly: on a patient platform, around a third reported a slight effect and almost all reported no side effects; at high amounts, nausea and diarrhea were the main issues. In Slovenia, the content of 19 out of 20 products tested did not match the label. Anyone taking blood thinners should discuss it with a doctor beforehand. Sirtuins remain a lively field of research, for example via NAD as the fuel of these enzymes.
