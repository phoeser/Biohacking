Podcast episode 48 (in German)

Berberine: nature’s metformin fact-checked

August 19, 2026

Berberine, a yellow plant compound from barberry and Chinese goldthread, held its own against metformin in a comparative study. The nickname “nature’s metformin” is therefore surprisingly fair, whereas “Nature’s Ozempic” is not. The episode sorts out what holds up about berberine.

What it is about

Berberine is a bitter alkaloid that Traditional Chinese Medicine has used for millennia against infections of the digestive tract. In the 1980s, Chinese doctors noticed that blood sugar also fell in diarrhea patients with diabetes; this gave rise to dozens of clinical studies. The mechanism resembles that of metformin: berberine activates AMPK, the cell’s energy sensor, and simulates a shortage as during fasting. The episode goes through the studies on blood sugar, cholesterol and PCOS and explains the role of the gut microbiome and the interactions with medications. At the end stands a well-studied metabolic aid for prediabetes, not a substitute for diagnosis and treatment, and not an appetite suppressant.

Key points

  • In the study by Yin, Xing and Ye, published in 2008 in Metabolism, newly diagnosed type 2 diabetics improved on berberine over 3 months practically as much as on metformin; HbA1c fell by about 2 percentage points.
  • Meta-analyses confirm the direction: across studies, long-term blood sugar falls by about 0.5 to just under 1 percent, and insulin sensitivity and fasting values improve markedly.
  • In meta-analyses, LDL cholesterol falls by around 20 milligrams per deciliter, along with triglycerides; to do this, berberine throttles the protein PCSK9 and stabilizes the liver’s LDL receptors.
  • Only a few percent reach the blood; gut bacteria convert berberine into the more readily absorbed dihydroberberine, and the microbiome shifts measurably toward beneficial bacteria.
  • In meta-analyses, berberine accounts on average for about 2 to 3 kilos of weight, far from the 15 to 20 percent of the GLP-1 injections.
  • Berberine inhibits the liver enzymes CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, through which many medications are broken down, from blood thinners to immunosuppressants.
  • In worms and flies, berberine extends life, and in mice it improves metabolic aging; metformin itself is to be tested as an anti-aging candidate in the TAME trial.

Where the data stop

Most studies come from China, are small and run for only months; large Western long-term studies are lacking. Metformin brings decades of safety data from millions of patients, which berberine does not have. The longevity hope rests on animal data, and whether the fasting mimetic dampens adaptation to training is unresolved. The evidence on the further developed dihydroberberine is also still young.

At the start, berberine can upset the gut, with bloating, soft stools or nausea. The interactions are more important: anyone who takes medication regularly should clarify the combination with a doctor, and together with diabetes medication blood sugar can drop too low. Berberine is off-limits during pregnancy and breastfeeding. In diabetes, treatment belongs in the hands of a physician, in PCOS in those of a gynecologist. The foundation remains: muscles, walks after meals, fewer sugary drinks.
