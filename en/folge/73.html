Podcast episode 73 (in German)

Whole-body hyperthermia & infrared: heat therapy fact-checked

September 11, 2026

Of all the methods in this series, heat sounds most like wellness and, of all things, has some of the cleanest studies. The episode sorts out sauna, infrared cabin and medical whole-body hyperthermia and shows where controlled artificial fever is really supported.

What it is about

The episode first sets up a heat ladder: the classic sauna heats the air to 80 to 90 degrees and mainly stimulates the skin, the infrared cabin works at an air temperature of 40 to 60 degrees and warms the tissue directly, and whole-body hyperthermia raises the core temperature in a targeted way under supervision. For this, water-filtered infrared A is predominantly used today, in which a layer of water filters out the components that stress the skin. After that it is about the evidence: a randomized, sham-controlled depression study in one of the toughest psychiatry journals, randomized data on fibromyalgia, clinically established wound healing — and Finnish sauna epidemiology as the foundation. Finally, the episode draws the dividing line to oncological hyperthermia.

Key points

  • The heat ladder in numbers: sauna at 80 to 90 degrees air temperature, infrared cabin at 40 to 60 degrees, whole-body hyperthermia with a core temperature of 38 to 39 degrees.
  • A randomized, sham-controlled study published in JAMA Psychiatry in 2016 found a clinically relevant improvement in depression scores after a single session, lasting over 6 weeks.
  • Medical whole-body hyperthermia holds the elevated core temperature for 1 to 2 hours, monitored and with water-filtered infrared A as the heat source.
  • The Finnish KIHD cohort followed over 2,000 men for more than 20 years and shows a textbook dose-response curve.
  • Those who used the sauna 4 to 7 times a week had, compared with once-a-week users, around half the risk of sudden cardiac death and about 40 percent lower all-cause mortality.
  • Dementia and Alzheimer’s risk was two thirds lower among frequent sauna users — observational data, but remarkably consistent across the dose.
  • The supported free way to start is a hot bath at 40 degrees for 20 minutes, 1 to 2 hours before sleep.

Where the data stop

The depression study is the episode’s big bang and at the same time its greatest uncertainty: it is small, replications are still under way, and a single session with six weeks of after-effect is a finding that needs confirmation. The Finnish data, in turn, are observational data — Finns do not use the sauna in a randomized way, and those who go four times a week probably differ in other respects too from those who go once.

Oncological hyperthermia, with its considerably higher target temperatures, must also be kept strictly separate: it is a hospital procedure as an addition to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with its own body of studies and its own risks, and it has no place in a wellness context. The mild variant does not suit everyone either. Strain on the circulation is the main point, and unstable heart disease, very low blood pressure, recent thromboses, pregnancy and medications that interfere with temperature regulation should be discussed with a doctor beforehand.
