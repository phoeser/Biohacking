Podcast episode 6 (in German)

Retatrutide: the most powerful weight-loss drug of the future fact-checked

July 12, 2026

Retatrutide operates 3 hormone switches at once, and since late September 2026 the phase 3 trial TRIUMPH-1 has been published after peer review: on the highest dose level, participants lost 25 percent of their weight after 80 weeks, versus 3.9 percent on placebo. The episode explains the triagonist and shows why gray-market products are far removed from these figures.

What it is about

Retatrutide from Eli Lilly, nicknamed “Triple G” in the scene, is a triagonist: semaglutide operates one hormone switch, GLP-1, tirzepatide two, GLP-1 and GIP, and retatrutide adds the glucagon receptor as a third. The first two curb appetite and smooth blood sugar; glucagon is meant to boost energy expenditure and mobilize fat, especially in the liver. The episode starts with “food noise”, the constant circling of thoughts around food, then goes through the phase 2 trial, the liver data, a phase 3 trial in type 2 diabetes and the TRIUMPH series, and sets them against what analyses of gray-market users show. At the end stands a well-supported molecule that is not yet approved anywhere and whose marketing has run ahead of the data.

Key points

  • In the phase 2 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine from 2023 with 338 adults, participants on the highest dose level lost 17.5 percent of their weight after 24 weeks, versus 1.6 percent on placebo; after 48 weeks it was 24.2 versus 2.1 percent, 83 percent had lost at least 15 percent, and the curve had not yet reached a plateau.
  • In the liver substudy with 98 participants, liver fat measured by magnetic resonance fell after 24 weeks by 42.9 to 82.4 percent depending on the dose level, while it rose by 0.3 percent on placebo; on the highest dose level, 86 percent reached a normal liver fat content, on placebo nobody did.
  • A peer-reviewed phase 3 trial in The Lancet in type 2 diabetes with 537 people over 40 weeks: long-term blood sugar fell by up to 1.94 percentage points, versus 0.81 on placebo, and weight by up to 15.3 percent; there were no severe hypoglycemic episodes.
  • TRIUMPH-1 followed 2,339 people without diabetes for 80 weeks. Under the stricter analysis that also counts dropouts, those on the highest dose level lost 25 percent versus 3.9 percent on placebo, published after peer review in the New England Journal of Medicine since late September 2026; for everyone who continued as planned, Eli Lilly reported 28.3 percent in May.
  • According to the manufacturer, the other TRIUMPH trials point in the same direction: 28.7 percent in knee osteoarthritis, 22.6 percent in cardiovascular disease, 20.8 percent in diabetes with overweight.
  • As a report: many people using the new weight-loss injections describe that the constant circling of thoughts around food, the “food noise”, becomes quieter; GLP-1 acts as far as brain regions that control hunger and reward. This is not a measured endpoint.
  • The BK-Score sums this up as “Well supported, heavily overhyped”: 9 out of 10 for the human data, 8 for the mechanism, 6 for the safety data, and 4 each for track record of use and hype gap.

Where the data stop

Nobody has tested separately how much the glucagon arm contributes on its own, and there is no head-to-head comparison with tirzepatide; the claim that retatrutide is stronger rests on placing different trials side by side. The other TRIUMPH trials are so far known only from manufacturer announcements. The side effects resemble those of the GLP-1 family but are more pronounced: in TRIUMPH-1, 28.6 to 42.4 percent had nausea depending on the dose level, versus 14.8 percent on placebo; on the highest dose level, 11.3 percent stopped because of side effects, versus 4.9 percent on placebo, and in TRIUMPH-4 as many as 18.2 percent. New is dysesthesia, a tingling or abnormal sensation of the skin, at 12.5 versus 0.9 percent. The glucagon arm raises the heart rate, and long-term data are lacking.

Gray-market products are not the trial drug. In a not yet peer-reviewed analysis of 29 million US patient records, the source of retatrutide could be traced for 531 people, and 71.2 percent obtained it outside a trial. They lost 7.2 percent of their weight after 6 to 12 months, trial participants in the same analysis 15.5 percent, comparable tirzepatide users 7.7 percent; their heart rate rose by 2.5 beats per minute. A case report describes impending ketoacidosis in type 1 diabetes after retatrutide bought online. Retatrutide is not approved anywhere in the world; Eli Lilly plans to file with the FDA in the first quarter of 2027. In Germany it is not a marketable medicine, the FDA warns against products labeled “for research purposes only”, and in sport it is on the WADA prohibited list. Anyone considering the topic for themselves should discuss it with a doctor.
