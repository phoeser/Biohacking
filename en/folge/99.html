Podcast episode 99 (in German)

Micronutrient concentrates – nine out of ten for evidence, and still a no

October 7, 2026

In July, a juice costing 100 euros per liter won a consumer award that nobody wants to win. But the award is not the interesting part — rather, that this product class is among the best studied ever discussed here.

What it is about

Liquid micronutrient concentrates consist of fruit juice concentrate with vegetable and herb extracts; the vitamin amounts on the label come from isolated, added pure substances. So the question is whether a multivitamin does anything for a healthy person — and there are large, blinded trials on that. The episode first shows what they found on the positive side, for cataract, memory, fatigue and epigenetic clocks, listens to what users report, and then explains why heart, cancer and lifespan do not follow for healthy people. At the end comes a warning about vitamin K that is rarely given.

Key points

  • In the Physicians' Health Study II, 14,641 physicians took a multivitamin or placebo for eleven years: 9 percent less cataract and 8 percent fewer total cancers.
  • In COSMOS-Web with 3,562 older adults, memory was the prespecified primary endpoint, and it was met; across 3 COSMOS substudies combined, the benefit was 0.07 standard deviations.
  • A COSMOS analysis in Nature Medicine 2026 with 958 participants found that two epigenetic clocks ticked more slowly under the multivitamin, by about 1.5 to 2.5 months per year; whether that means a longer life is open.
  • In 8 placebo-controlled studies in healthy people, participants felt less stressed, anxious and fatigued under vitamins; at a Berlin university hospital, a fruit-and-vegetable concentrate brought about one fifth fewer days with moderate to severe colds.
  • For heart, cancer and lifespan, the big numbers do not follow for healthy people: 21 randomized studies with 91,000 people give 0.98 for all-cause mortality, 3 US cohorts with 390,000 adults show no survival benefit, and COSMOS missed its primary cancer endpoint.
  • For the market leader itself there is 1 placebo-controlled study with 159 participants: 48 lab values measured, 20 published as comparisons, 3 favorable, including homocysteine.
  • The BK-Score gives human evidence 9, track record of use 9, safety data 8 and hype gap 8; the direction is negative because heart, cancer and lifespan do not follow for healthy people.

Where the data stop

Here the boundary runs differently than usual: there are plenty of data, but for heart, cancer and lifespan they show no benefit for healthy people. The US Preventive Services Task Force evaluated 84 studies with 740,000 people and, for multivitamins, arrives at the most cautious sentence an agency can say — the evidence is insufficient to weigh benefit and harm against each other. That is not proof of efficacy and does not contradict the large studies, but rates them more strictly. The advertised better absorption also remains open: studies of liquid versus solid measure blood levels over 6 hours in fasting people; there is no randomized study with a health outcome on this.

One warning, on the other hand, applies very concretely. The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment sees a measurable effect on blood clotting from 150 micrograms of vitamin K1 and from 45 micrograms of K2. Anyone taking Marcumar or warfarin discusses every product containing vitamin K with a physician beforehand; the problem is less the amount than the jump.
