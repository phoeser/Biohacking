Podcast episode 77 (in German)

Alcohol: the pleasurable poison fact-checked

September 15, 2026

We measure our sleep, optimize our mitochondria, count grams of protein — and on Friday evening two glasses are on the table. This solo episode looks, without moralizing and without whitewashing, at what the data on alcohol now show.

What it is about

For decades, observational studies showed a J-shaped curve: moderate drinkers seemed to live longer than abstainers. The episode shows where the catch in this curve lies, what Mendelian randomization made of it and why the major institutions rewrote their recommendations. Then it goes into the body — acetaldehyde, cancer, sleep, muscle building, hormones — and after that to the good news, which is surprisingly strong: how quickly measurable values improve when you give the body a break. Finally there is a toolkit for everyone who wants to drink less without imposing an ascetic dogma on themselves.

Key points

  • The group of abstainers contains many sick quitters; if moderate drinkers are compared only with lifelong non-drinkers, the supposed protective effect shrinks dramatically.
  • A Mendelian randomization study in a Chinese biobank with more than 500,000 adults found no protective effect of moderate drinking: stroke risk rose steadily with the genetically predicted amount, while no clear association was seen for heart attack.
  • WHO Europe wrote in 2023 that there is no safe amount; Canada cites at most 2 standard drinks per week, and the German Nutrition Society has said since 2024 that zero is best.
  • Acetaldehyde is in the highest carcinogen category, the same as tobacco smoke and asbestos, and 7 types of cancer are causally linked to alcohol.
  • In a Finnish study, even a little alcohol lowered the recovery value calculated from heart rate variability in the first hours of sleep by just over 9 percentage points, a lot of alcohol by almost 40. In wearable data, one drink above one’s own average raised the nightly resting heart rate by just under 2.5 to 3 beats, and the first dream sleep comes later.
  • An Australian study found muscle protein synthesis after exercise reduced by 24 percent, even when protein was eaten at the same time — but in 8 men after an average of 12 standard drinks; it says nothing about a single glass.
  • In a British study, one month without alcohol reduced insulin resistance by a quarter and systolic blood pressure by just over 6 percent; in a survey after Dry January, just over half said they slept better.

Where the data stop

The risk curve rises with the amount, but it starts flat. One glass a week moves the absolute lifetime risk only minimally, and the Canadian guidelines call up to 2 drinks per week a low risk. The individual overall picture counts: someone who is lean, trains, sleeps well and consciously has a glass twice a month has a different risk profile from the daily after-work drinker. Science does not say that one sip ruins everything — it says that there is no health reason to drink and that every reduction counts.

The numbers from wearables are also everyday data, not controlled studies. Anyone taking medication or supplements should do the interaction check with a doctor or pharmacy; it takes 5 minutes. And anyone who notices that stopping is hard, drinks secretly or thinks about drinking in the morning will find confidential support from their GP, addiction counselling services and anonymous groups.
