Podcast episode 83 (in German)

Senolytics off the shelf – fisetin, quercetin and the study that has been running for eight years

September 21, 2026

A dye yellow from the wood of the smoke bush, already known to Pliny the Elder, now stands on every longevity shelf: fisetin. The episode tells how the dye became a hope of aging research – and what studies in mice and humans have shown so far.

What it is about

Senescent cells no longer divide, but they do not die either: they linger, release inflammatory substances and infect healthy neighbors. Senolytics are meant to clear them out selectively, and the principle has been cleanly demonstrated in mice, and in humans with the combination of dasatinib and quercetin. The episode follows the path of fisetin: the 2018 mouse study that made the field famous, the independent testing program that could not confirm it, the first small studies in humans, reports from users and the Mayo study AFFIRM, whose main part is due to be completed in November 2026 according to the registry.

Key points

  • In 2018, a group led by Laura Niedernhofer and Paul Robbins tested ten plant compounds on aged cells; fisetin was the strongest. Old mice that received it only late in life had fewer senescence markers and lived longer.
  • In humans, clearing aged cells has been shown with dasatinib plus quercetin: in nine people with diabetic kidney disease, fewer senescent cells were found in fat and skin eleven days after three days of treatment. In 60 postmenopausal women, the combination missed its primary goal, but a bone formation marker rose by 16 percent, and in the women with the most senescent cells, bone density at the forearm increased.
  • For fisetin alone, there are small randomized studies: in 44 men with obesity, interleukin 6, TNF-alpha and insulin resistance fell over twelve weeks, most clearly with additional training; in 37 colorectal cancer patients on chemotherapy, interleukin 8 fell more than on placebo, and stroke patients treated late had better neurological scores.
  • An osteoarthritis study with 75 participants over about a year found no difference in side effects (28 on fisetin, 33 on placebo) or inflammation markers. A review from September 2026 counts 34 registered fisetin studies, only four of them with results so far.
  • In the independent US testing program in genetically mixed mice, animals given fisetin did not live longer, and senescence markers in liver, kidney and brain did not fall – with a different mouse strain, a different amount and a different route; the authors explicitly limit their result to these conditions.
  • AFFIRM at the Mayo Clinic is testing fisetin in 40 women aged 70 and over, mainly for walking ability; it began in February 2018, with estimated completion of the primary endpoint in November 2026 and of the whole study in November 2027.
  • Quercetin has never been tested on its own as a senolytic in humans, only together with dasatinib. Its strongest story of its own is blood pressure: in three meta-analyses, the upper value fell by two to three points, even though absorption varies between 0 and more than 50 percent of the amount swallowed.

Where the data stop

The first fisetin study is not refuted by the testing program, but the question is open again. In humans, clearing aged cells has so far been shown only with the combination of dasatinib and quercetin – dasatinib is a cancer drug that belongs exclusively in the hands of a physician. Whether fisetin from a capsule can do the same has not yet been shown: the small fisetin studies measure inflammation markers, not senescent cells. User reports are contradictory, and in a small pilot study with ten adults, measured biological age fell in four and rose in five. The BK score gives fisetin 4 points for human evidence and 1 for the hype gap, because marketing words such as cell renewal run far ahead of the evidence. In the EU, isolated high-purity fisetin has been classified as a non-authorized novel food since April 2026.

Anyone taking blood thinners should clarify larger amounts of flavonoids with a doctor beforehand, because both can run through the same liver enzymes; this has not been studied in humans. Anyone who has just had surgery or has a wound that is healing should wait – that is when senescent cells are actually useful. The best-supported senolytic available without a prescription remains exercise: a twelve-week training program lowered senescence markers in immune cells and blood in older people.
