Podcast episode 72 (in German)

Heart rate variability (HRV): the stress compass fact-checked

September 10, 2026

Millions of people measure their heart rate variability every night, and very few know what the number actually says. This episode resolves the paradox of why an irregularly beating heart is the healthier one, and shows how to read the value without going crazy.

What it is about

Heart rate variability measures the micro-fluctuations between two heartbeats and thus shows how well the accelerator and brake of the autonomic nervous system work together. High variability means: the vagal brake is strong, the system responds flexibly to demands. The episode explains the two metrics you need to know for this and then sets out its most important rule: absolute values are useless for comparisons between people, because the spread among healthy people is enormous. Only your own baseline counts. After that it is about trends instead of daily drama, the list of lowering and raising factors, HRV as an early warning system for infections, and resonance breathing as the most direct line to the vagus nerve.

Key points

  • Between two heartbeats there are sometimes 900, sometimes 1,050, sometimes 980 milliseconds — this fine fluctuation is the measured value, and more of it is the better sign.
  • RMSSD is the nightly short-term value of almost all wearables and reflects almost purely the vagal brake; SDNN captures the overall fluctuation over longer periods.
  • Nightly RMSSD values of healthy adults range roughly between 20 and over 100 milliseconds: a forty-year-old with 35 can be very fit, a twenty-year-old with 60 stressed.
  • Your own baseline emerges over 2 to 4 weeks of measurement; after that, the 7-day average is the truth and the single value just weather.
  • Even 2 glasses of alcohol in the evening measurably depress nightly recovery — in the episode, alcohol counts as the most reliable HRV-lowering factor of all.
  • Resonance breathing at about 6 breaths per minute, that is 5 seconds in and 5 seconds out, acutely and measurably raises HRV; 5 to 10 minutes daily is the study format.
  • The episode’s protocol runs over 4 weeks: 2 weeks of measuring only, in week 3 add exactly one raising factor, in week 4 read the 7-day average.

Where the data stop

The link between low HRV and higher cardiovascular risk is among the more robust findings in cardiology, but it remains an association. The data do not say that a trained HRV automatically means a longer life. And the number itself is sensitive: genetics, age, fitness and measurement method vary so widely that comparisons between people yield nothing. Chest straps are considered the gold standard; the optical sensors of rings and watches are now decent at night, but to be taken with caution during the day.

The second limit is psychological. Anyone who takes a red value every morning as a reason for drama has turned the measuring device into a stressor; the warning about orthosomnia from the sleep episode applies doubly to HRV apps. And palpitations, skipped beats or persistently abnormal values belong in a cardiological work-up — HRV does not replace an ECG.
