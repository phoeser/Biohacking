Podcast episode 113 (in German)

Ten thousand steps – a product name from nineteen sixty-five

October 21, 2026

The 10,000 steps come from the name of a pedometer from 1965, not from a study. An analysis of 57 studies suggests a lower mark — and for once the tested figure is the more comfortable one.

What it is about

The pedometer Manpo-kei, translated ten-thousand-steps meter, was launched by the Japanese company Yamasa in 1965 with the slogan “Health with 10,000 steps a day”. No authority ever recommended the figure. The episode sets against it a preregistered analysis from Lancet Public Health from August 2025, in which all steps were measured with devices and none were self-reported. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 are associated with markedly lower mortality, cardiovascular disease, dementia and falls. For several endpoints the curve already bends at 5,000 to 7,000 steps. The conclusion: the first additional steps bring the most, and anyone who already walks more should not stop.

Key points

  • The analysis covers 57 studies from 35 cohorts, of which 31 studies from 24 cohorts entered the calculations, on a total of 9 endpoints.
  • Compared with 2,000 steps, the hazard ratio for all-cause mortality at 7,000 steps was 0.53, confidence interval 0.46 to 0.60, from 14 studies — 47 percent lower.
  • For cardiovascular disease the figure was 0.75, that is, 25 percent lower, for dementia 0.62, for falls 0.72, for depressive symptoms 0.78 and for type 2 diabetes 0.86.
  • For new cancer cases the value was 0.94 with a confidence interval of 0.87 to 1.01; because 1 lies within it, this finding is not established.
  • For all-cause mortality, new cardiovascular disease, dementia and falls the curve flattens at around 5,000 to 7,000 steps; going from 2,000 to 5,000 gains more than going from 8,000 to 11,000.
  • Behind mortality there are 14 studies, behind dementia and new cancer cases 2 each, behind depressive symptoms 3 and behind falls 4.
  • A count of 1,247 evaluable forum comments names habit, at 30 percent, as the most common strategy for sticking with it, a goal at 13.9 percent, a counting app at 2.8 percent.

Where the data stop

All figures come from observational studies: people were given a device, nobody was assigned steps. People who walk a lot are often leaner, less often ill, less often poor and less often alone. This can be adjusted for, but never completely, and the authors themselves name this residual confounding as a limitation, as well as the fact that an analysis by age group was not possible. They rate the certainty as moderate for most endpoints, as low for cardiovascular mortality, new cancer cases and physical function, and as very low for falls — of all things, the 28 percent fewer falls were what made the headlines.

The 7,000 are not an upper limit. For cardiovascular mortality, cancer mortality, diabetes and depressive symptoms, the curve continues without a bend in the measured range, and the authors explicitly consider 10,000 a reasonable goal for more active people. The forum count is not representative and supports none of these figures. And the measurements were taken in people in everyday life, not in people recovering from illness.
