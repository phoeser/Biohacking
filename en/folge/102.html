Podcast episode 102 (in German)

Intermittent fasting after menopause – the study with an average age of 32

October 10, 2026

Why do women in midlife in particular so often turn to the eating window, and what does it bring them? Paula and Paul argue amicably about calorie brake and clock time, present the studies in women during the transition and ask where the data for this group are still thin.

What it is about

Around menopause, women gain weight on average, and fat tends to move to the abdomen. For weight, intermittent fasting works as well as an equal calorie reduction, not better; the time of day itself can, however, influence metabolism. The episode presents the studies in women during and after the transition, listens to users, examines the worry about hormones and explains why the best long-term data come from other groups and what to keep in mind for bones, muscles and diabetes medication.

Key points

  • In the Nurses' Health Study II, just over 38,000 women gained 800 grams a year on average around menopause; in the most-read posts of the Reddit menopause forum, about one in five is about weight.
  • For weight, the eating window performs as well as an equal calorie reduction, not better; in men with prediabetes, an early six-hour window improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure even without weight loss.
  • In Poznań, 62 women aged 41 to 61 did 12 weeks of circuit training, some of them also eating within an eight-hour window: fat mass, waist and insulin resistance fell only in that group, and on the menopause rating scale the window group ended almost 5 of 44 points better, mainly for mood, irritability and exhaustion.
  • In Hong Kong, the group with window plus endurance training lost almost 3 kilos more fat than the control group, and more than 80 percent stuck to the plan; in two studies from Chicago, estrogens and progesterone remained unchanged in postmenopausal women.
  • In everyday life the window is more of a railing than a cage: in Poznań, 8 hours were achieved on just over 60 percent of days, 9 hours on just over 85 percent.
  • The BK-Score for intermittent fasting stands at human evidence 7, safety data 8 and track record of use 9; for women in menopause alone the data would be thinner, because most studies were done in other groups.

Where the data stop

The best long-term study on the time window ran for one year in China with 139 people with obesity, around 32 years old on average; a large one-year study on alternate-day fasting even excluded women in perimenopause. Studies in women in menopause mostly run 12 weeks or less. For bone, an analysis of 7 studies averaging ten and a half weeks shows no change in bone density, short and not targeted at this group. A study in 101 postmenopausal women without any window shows where the risk lies instead: those who cut very sharply lost more lean mass and more hip bone density than with moderate restriction. That is why protein and strength training belong with it.

Anyone taking insulin or older insulin-releasing diabetes tablets discusses fasting with a physician first; in a 5:2 study in type 2 diabetes, the medication protocol had to be changed after the 38th person because of hypoglycemia. And anyone who feels persistently worse with the window may read that as a signal. New studies are underway, for example in Beirut on mood in women in perimenopause.
