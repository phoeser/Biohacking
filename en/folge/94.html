Podcast episode 94 (in German)

AOD-9604 – thoroughly studied, and still a no

October 2, 2026

AOD-9604 is the rare exception in this series: a peptide that fails not because human data are missing, but because of the data that exist. 893 people, six randomized studies — and still a no. The reason for that is the most interesting part.

What it is about

Growth hormone breaks down fat and at the same time makes tissue grow. AOD-9604 is the attempt to keep only the first effect: an Australian company split off 16 amino acids from the rear end of the molecule and turned them into a drug of its own. Between 2001 and 2006 it was tested in six randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies in 893 mostly overweight adults. The episode separates two results that are regularly mixed up in advertising: the safety data are good, the weight loss did not work. The large 24-week study missed its primary endpoint; in February 2007 the company ended the weight-loss program. One question that matters today remains open: all efficacy trials used tablets or capsules, while AOD-9604 is mostly offered as an injection today. Today the same substance is sold as a dietary supplement — with exactly these safety data as the argument.

Key points

  • Six randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies between 2001 and 2006 tested AOD-9604 in 893 adults — more investigation than 99 percent of the substances in this database have ever seen.
  • Safety could not be distinguished from placebo: no serious side effects, no increase in IGF-1, no antibody formation and no impaired glucose handling over up to 24 weeks.
  • A medium-sized 12-week study initially looked useful: according to company statements, around 2.8 kilograms of weight loss versus 0.8 kilograms on placebo, best at the lowest dose level.
  • The decisive large study with 536 participants over 24 weeks missed its primary endpoint; in February 2007 the company discontinued the program because the results did not support commercial success.
  • The early trials gave the substance as an intravenous infusion, and all efficacy trials used tablets or capsules; according to the FDA, there are no human data for the injection under the skin that is common today.
  • This large study was never published in a peer-reviewed journal — a 2013 review gives 536 participants, a second source 534.
  • In the US the substance is generally recognized as safe and may therefore be included in dietary supplements; that is a statement about safety, not about efficacy.
  • AOD-9604 is on the World Anti-Doping Agency’s prohibited list and can be reliably detected in urine; the BK-Score stands at human evidence 5, safety data 5, track record of use 5 and hype gap 1 with a negative direction. Track record of use, still 2 in the episode, was raised afterwards: at an FDA hearing in December 2024, around 159,000 compounded prescriptions in one year across nine US pharmacies were reported.

Where the data stop

What is missing here is not the investigation but its verifiability. The study on which the program failed was never published in a peer-reviewed journal. Even the number of participants is known only second-hand: once 536, once 534. That does not change the result, because the discontinuation of the program is documented by the company's announcement and by FDA records. But a study that nobody can read counts only by half — and that is exactly why the evidence axis shows a 5 and no higher number.

The second limit is time. The safety profile was good up to 24 weeks, for infusion and oral preparations. What happens beyond that, nobody has measured, and for injection under the skin human data are missing entirely. And for healthy people without clinical overweight there are no data anyway, because the studies were carried out in a different group.
