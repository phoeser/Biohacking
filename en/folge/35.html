Lithium – the trace element for the brain: longevity, mood and neuroprotection

August 9, 2026

Most people know lithium as a heavy-duty psychiatric drug. In tiny amounts, however, it could be a trace element our brain needs: in 2025, a Harvard paper in Nature linked a lack of lithium in the brain to the development of Alzheimer’s disease.

What it is about

The episode separates 2 worlds: high-dose lithium as a medicine, used for decades in psychiatry, for example in bipolar disorder, and microdoses that are around 1,000 times smaller. At the center is the Harvard paper according to which lithium is a natural component of the brain and people with incipient dementia have markedly less of it in the frontal cortex. Amyloid deposits soak up lithium like a sponge; a form called lithium orotate escapes this. Added to this are the mechanisms of action in the cell, population data on lithium in drinking water and the debate about whether lithium is a forgotten nutrient. What remains is one of the most optimistic stories in the longevity field, which so far rests mainly on animal models and observational data.

Key points

  • In the Harvard paper published in Nature in 2025, lithium deprivation alone triggered an Alzheimer’s-like disease in mice, with amyloid, tau tangles, inflammation, synapse loss and cognitive decline.
  • Lithium orotate was not captured by the amyloid clumps; in the drinking water of old mice with advanced disease, the damage reversed and memory returned.
  • In the cell, lithium inhibits the enzyme GSK-3, a main driver of tau tangling, promotes the nerve growth factor BDNF and autophagy, and has an anti-inflammatory effect.
  • Population studies from Japan to Texas tend to find fewer dementia cases and fewer suicides in regions with more natural lithium in the drinking water.
  • Lithium orotate has been sold as a dietary supplement for over 40 years; according to the episode, no kidney damage has been observed in the low range.
  • Microdoses are around 1,000 times smaller than the psychiatric doses; the known side effects on the thyroid and kidneys belong to the high-dose world.

Where the data stop

The spectacular findings so far come from animal models and observational data. That lithium orotate reversed damage in mice does not yet mean it prevents or treats Alzheimer’s in humans, and the drinking-water studies show associations, not proof. Human studies on microdosing are only at the beginning. Some sharper claims, up to the thesis of a “lithium conspiracy”, go further than the data support today.

Caution and consultation with a physician apply in kidney disease, with diuretics, during pregnancy and when medications for the nervous system are already being taken. Microdose lithium is not a substitute for treatment of a serious illness. And the basics come first: sleep, exercise, diet.
